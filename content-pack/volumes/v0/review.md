# Abstract

Neurostimulation and neuromodulation name a set of methods that share almost no physics: fields, pressure waves, light, heat, implanted electrodes, and molecules delivered by half a dozen routes. Reviews of the non-invasive techniques already treat electric, magnetic, ultrasonic and optical drives as four separate physical problems [30], and the clinical literature runs from non-invasive stimulation to implanted devices as a further spectrum [31]. This volume argues that they nonetheless share one description — a drive, a coupling mechanism, a cellular response, a subcellular and molecular cascade, a circuit effect, a systemic effect, an outcome — and it works through what is established, inferred and disputed at each link.

Three findings shape the volume. First, dose is not intensity: the normative definition covers every parameter of the configuration that shapes the delivered field [9], and yet a survey of 202 transcranial studies found 308 distinct electric-field outcome measures and no agreed metric [14]. Second, coupling is geometric: applied fields act first on axons and terminals, polarization depends on orientation, and the field reachable in a human brain — about 0.8 V/m at 2 mA, measured intracranially [40] — sits at or below thresholds measured in rodents [50]. Third, the acting cause is often not the nominal one: the ligand that drives a chemogenetic experiment is a metabolite of the compound injected [29], part of the human ultrasound literature is explained by an auditory pathway rather than a cortical one [77], and psychedelic plasticity depends on which receptor compartment is engaged [92].

<!-- synthesis -->
Those three findings generalise into the volume's method: state the level, state the species and preparation, state the dose as reported, and keep rival explanations attached to the claim rather than resolved silently. Twenty-three live disputes are catalogued on that basis, each with its rivals, the measurement that divides them and what would settle it. The closing section turns the volume's requirements into a proposed graph schema, names the ontologies that can anchor each level, and identifies the one layer where the field has a consensus reporting checklist [193] but nothing to reuse as an ontology: stimulation protocols and device parameters.

<!-- framing -->
This is a scope-bounded commissioned review rather than a systematic one, written by Daniel Adamek with AI assistance and not peer reviewed. Its literature sweep closed on 2026-09-15 and rests on 263 references, 260 of which were verified against a read abstract or full text. Volume 1 takes the first modality family in detail: vagus and cranial nerve stimulation.

## 1. What neuromodulation is, and what the word hides

<!-- framing -->
This section sets out how the word "neuromodulation" is used, where those uses collide, and which usage the rest of the volume adopts.

### Three referents for one word

"Neuromodulation" is used for three different things. The first is a delivered drive: a current, a field or an agent that someone applies to a nervous system. The second is the physiological change that follows from that drive. The third is a class of endogenous substances, the neuromodulators, and the effects they have on neurons and circuits. The clinical societies use the word mostly in the first two senses [1,3]. Cellular and circuit neuroscience uses it in the third [5].

<!-- synthesis -->
The first two senses are often merged in a single sentence, because a definition that says a therapy "alters nerve activity" names both the act and its result. That merge is harmless in a clinic. It is not harmless in a knowledge graph, where the drive, the change it causes and the endogenous transmitter must be separate nodes joined by edges that carry evidence [1,3,5,9].

### What the clinical bodies say

The 2022 glossary produced jointly by the Neuromodulation Foundation, the Institute of Neuromodulation and the International Neuromodulation Society (INS) is the most detailed consensus source [1]. It extended a 2007 glossary written for spinal cord stimulation to peripheral nerve, dorsal root ganglion, deep brain and motor cortex stimulation, and defined 91 terms [1]. Its definition of neuromodulation is broad. Neuromodulation modifies the activity of neurons and glia by delivering an electrical, magnetic or chemical stimulus to a specific neurological target [1]. Two features matter here. The definition names glia as well as neurons, and it includes chemical delivery. The document is expert consensus, not an empirical study, and its scope is implanted systems [1].

The INS's public definition, written for a lay audience, is narrower in one respect and wider in another [3]. It describes neuromodulation as technology that acts directly on nerves, altering nerve activity by delivering electrical or pharmaceutical agents to a target area, and it explicitly counts implanted drug pumps [3]. It does not mention magnetic stimulation or glia. It is a web page whose wording may change, so this volume cites it with its access date of 2026-09-15 [3].

A third consensus paper, from an international expert group led by Sivanesan and North, proposed its own definition of neuromodulation together with a classification of implantable electrical therapies for chronic pain [2]. It sorts those therapies by intended treatment site and clinical application, and by technical parameters that include waveform and dose [2]. It also notes that published studies often do not report the effective stimulation dose, so that they cannot be replicated [2]. That complaint returns in section 3, where Peterchev and colleagues' device-side definition of dose is set out [9]. Like the 2022 glossary, this paper is expert consensus, and its scope is limited to implants for pain [2].

The three sources can be compared point by point. On which drives count, the 2022 glossary names electrical, magnetic and chemical stimuli [1]. The INS web page names electrical and pharmaceutical agents [3]. The 2024 paper classifies implantable electrical therapies only [2]. On which cells are acted on, the 2022 glossary names neurons and glia [1], while the INS web page speaks only of nerves [3]. On dose, the 2024 paper makes waveform and dose part of its classification [2], and neither of the other two definitions mentions dose [1,3]. The 2022 glossary covers implanted systems [1] and the 2024 paper covers implants for pain [2]. The INS web page, while not limited to implants, names implanted drug pumps [3].

### Neurostimulation and neuromodulation

The two words are used side by side in the same documents. The 2022 consensus document is titled a glossary of *neurostimulation* terminology, yet the definition it gives is of *neuromodulation* [1]. The 2024 classification paper defines neuromodulation and then classifies implantable electrical *modulation* [2]. The naming problem also exists inside a single family of techniques. The transcranial electrical stimulation nomenclature paper had to settle names for tDCS, tACS, tPCS and newer variants. It weighed broad definitions, which leave room for new techniques, against the specific definitions needed to reproduce protocols and to compare safety and efficacy [4].

<!-- synthesis -->
A usage that fits these sources treats "stimulation" as the name of an act (a delivered electrical or magnetic drive) and "modulation" as the name of the aim or the result (a change in ongoing activity). On that reading, stimulation is one kind of drive and modulation is what one hopes it produces. The sources do not state this distinction in these words, and they do not apply it consistently [1,2,4]. This volume uses it because a graph needs the act and its result as separate nodes.

### The collision with the cellular sense

In cellular and circuit neuroscience, "neuromodulation" has an older and quite different referent. Marder's review describes neuromodulatory substances that reach neurons as local hormones, as cotransmitters released by projection neurons, and through the circulation [5]. They change neurons' intrinsic firing properties and the effective strength of synapses, and so reconfigure circuits and change behavioural output [5]. Much of that work comes from small invertebrate circuits, and the review draws on mixed preparations [5]. On this account the anatomical wiring diagram is only a scaffold, and the modulatory state decides which functional circuit is running [5].

The conflict between this sense and the clinical sense is about terminology, not about data [1,2,5]. A clinician's "neuromodulation" is something done *to* a patient. Marder's "neuromodulation" is something a nervous system does *to itself*, through substances it releases [5]. The two senses also meet in practice, and the neuroimmune work shows one place where they do. In rats, electrical stimulation of the peripheral vagus nerve during lethal endotoxaemia inhibited hepatic TNF synthesis and blunted peak serum TNF, and acetylcholine suppressed TNF release from LPS-stimulated human macrophages *in vitro* [148]. The authors identified efferent vagal signalling as a parasympathetic anti-inflammatory pathway, so an applied drive and an endogenous transmitter appear in the same reported mechanism [148].

### The usage this volume fixes, and its scope

<!-- synthesis -->
This volume uses "neuromodulation" for any exogenous drive that alters nerve activity and the chain of changes it sets off. It uses "neuromodulator" and "neuromodulator system" only in Marder's cellular sense, for endogenous substances and the neurons that release them [5]. Where a sentence could be read either way, the text names the level instead of relying on the word, and it keeps the collision visible because both uses are legitimate in their own literatures [1,3,5]. Under this usage devices and drugs are peers, since both are exogenous drives that alter nerve activity. The INS web page already counts pharmaceutical delivery and implanted drug pumps [3], the 2022 glossary counts chemical stimuli [1], and Tracey proposed that a hard-wired neural loop can be addressed by electrical or pharmacological means [149]; this volume extends that parity to drugs given by any route, which none of these sources does explicitly. It covers every human-applicable way of driving the nervous system from outside, device or drug. It traces each drive through the same levels, from how the drive couples to tissue to what changes in behaviour or disease. It leaves out device engineering, regulatory and reimbursement detail, practical clinical protocols and recreational drug use. Genetic actuators — optogenetic, chemogenetic, sonogenetic and magnetogenetic tools — get no chapter of their own; they appear only where they are the evidence for a mechanism, because they are laboratory instruments rather than drives that can be applied to a person. Readers who need the clinical picture of delivery, indications and ongoing trials, from non-invasive stimulation to deep brain, spinal cord and vagus nerve stimulation, can start from a recent review [31].

### Vocabulary standards the volume borrows

Other communities have faced the same naming problem and built shared vocabularies. The RATES reporting checklist for transcranial electrical stimulation came from a three-round Delphi study with 38 experts. It lists 66 items in five groups (participants, stimulator, electrodes, current, procedure) and has a 26-item essential version [193]. The Neuron Phenotype Ontology names neuron types by breaking them into phenotypes that carry identifiers from community ontologies. Tested against three published classifications of cortical neurons, it reconciled them with each other and with the informal names used in the literature [156]. The Evidence and Conclusion Ontology names the kinds of evidence that support an annotation or assertion [218]. The graph built from this volume uses these vocabularies, and section 15 returns to them.

### A first pass at the levels

<!-- synthesis -->
The rest of the volume writes every modality as a chain: drive → coupling → cellular → subcellular/molecular → circuit → system → outcome. Section 2 argues for the chain. Peterchev and colleagues separate the dose, meaning the device-side parameters an operator controls, from the field it produces in tissue and from the physiological effect [9]. That separation is the difference between *drive* and *coupling*. Ranck's review asks which neural elements an applied current actually excites in the mammalian CNS [34]. It reports chronaxies of about 50–100 µs for central myelinated axons, against 200–700 µs for some grey-matter stimulation, a difference that leaves open what is being excited [34]. That is a *cellular*-level question. Marder's account of modulators changing intrinsic and synaptic properties spans the *subcellular* and *circuit* levels [5]. Tracey's reflex, with an afferent limb, central integration and an efferent cholinergic limb, is a *system*-level description [149]. Borovikova and colleagues' rat experiment ran from a stimulation drive to a molecular endpoint (TNF) and to an *outcome* (prevention of shock) [148]. Section 2 defines each level. Here the point is only that the word "neuromodulation" is used at every one of them, and the text has to say which level it means.

## 2. Levels of organisation and the drive → outcome chain

<!-- framing -->
This section sets out the chain that organises the rest of the volume, carries one example along it, and names the places where it tends to break.

<!-- synthesis -->
The claim of this section is that every modality in the volume, device or drug, can be written as one chain: drive → coupling → cellular → subcellular/molecular → circuit → system → outcome. The drive is what is delivered. Coupling is the physical or chemical event by which the drive first changes tissue. The cellular level is what a neuron or glial cell does in response. The subcellular/molecular level is the signalling that follows, and that often outlasts the stimulus. The circuit level is how populations of connected cells change. The system level is how a whole body system, such as arousal, autonomic or immune control, changes. The outcome is what is measured in the organism. No cited paper states this seven-step chain. It is assembled from step-wise chains in two separate literatures: the brain-stimulation chain from induced electric field through local and network effects to behaviour [199], and the drug-development chain from exposure at the target site through target binding to pharmacological activity [24].

<!-- figure: fig1 -->

### Levels as a methodological commitment

Treating the chain as levels is a commitment about what counts as an explanation, not a metaphor. Churchland and Sejnowski argued that understanding perception, learning and consciousness would need methods that record large-scale brain activity together with computational models, and the article is widely cited for a scheme of levels of organisation running from molecules to systems, in which an explanation has to link levels rather than stay at one [6]. The Atlas chain is a levels-of-organisation scheme in that sense: each link names a physical scale and a kind of entity.

The commitment has consequences, because a level cannot be read off the one below it. Marder's review of neuromodulation of neuronal circuits, drawn largely from invertebrate small-circuit work, argues that neuromodulatory substances change neurons' intrinsic firing properties and effective synaptic strengths, so that the anatomical connectome is only a scaffold and the modulatory state decides which functional circuit operates [5]. If wiring alone does not fix circuit function, then a description of which cells a drive reaches does not fix its circuit effect. The circuit link has to be measured, or modelled, in its own right.

The same point holds for drugs. Franks's review of general anaesthesia argues that a small number of molecular targets account for the action of chemically diverse anaesthetics, and that the route from those targets to reversible loss of consciousness runs through the endogenous sleep and arousal circuitry, with the thalamus and the networks that regulate it at the centre [183]. Target and behaviour are separated by an existing physiological control circuit, not a new pathway [183].

Modelling offers one way to connect the levels explicitly. D'Angelo and Jirsa propose combining bottom-up biophysical models of neurons and synapses with top-down network models built from functional activity, embedding detailed microcircuit models in larger assemblies to link microscopic mechanisms to macroscopic signals [8]. It is a programme, not a finished bridge.

<!-- synthesis -->
Read together, these sources suggest that the chain is not a hierarchy in which the lower level fully determines the upper. It is a list of places where a separate measurement or model is needed. That is the sense in which the Atlas uses it [5,6,8,183].

### What each link means: a worked example

<!-- framing -->
The example carried through the links below is the vagal route to immune control. It is taken here only as far as this section's sources go; section 9 gives the molecular detail.

Tracey formalised the finding that cholinergic neurons inhibit acute inflammation as a reflex: the nervous system senses inflammatory status and regulates it in real time, with an afferent limb, central integration and an efferent cholinergic limb [149]. He argued that because the loop is hard-wired, it can be addressed selectively and reversibly by electrical or pharmacological means, and proposed this as a treatment strategy for inflammatory disease [149]. It is a review of mixed-species work, not a single experiment.

<!-- synthesis -->
Written as the Atlas chain, the example reads as follows. *Drive*: an electrical stimulus applied to the vagus nerve, or a drug acting on the same pathway, both of which Tracey names as ways into the loop [149]. *Coupling*: the event by which that stimulus first changes excitable tissue in the nerve; Tracey's review does not specify it, and for a device it belongs to the electromagnetic coupling of section 4. *Cellular*: activity in the efferent cholinergic limb [149]. *Subcellular/molecular*: cholinergic signalling onto the cells that produce cytokines, which the reflex framing treats as the effector step [149]. *Circuit*: the afferent–central–efferent loop, in which central integration sets how strongly the efferent limb fires [149]. *System*: the immune system, with cytokine level as the controlled variable, in the way heart rate is controlled by the cardiovascular reflexes [149]. *Outcome*: inflammation, and in the clinical case the course of an inflammatory disease [149].

The drug version of the chain has the same shape under different names. Morgan and colleagues analysed 44 Pfizer Phase II programmes and found that lack of efficacy was the main reason for failure, yet in 43% of cases it was unclear whether the mechanism had been adequately tested [24]. They proposed three pillars that a programme must show: exposure at the target site, target binding, and expression of pharmacological activity [24]. In Atlas terms, the administered dose is the drive, exposure and binding are coupling, and pharmacological activity is the first cellular or molecular read-out.

Optogenetics shows the chain in its most controlled form. Boyden and colleagues delivered channelrhodopsin-2, a light-gated cation channel from algae, to cultured mammalian neurons by lentivirus, and showed that brief light pulses gave millisecond-timescale control of spiking and of excitatory and inhibitory synaptic transmission [90]. Here the coupling step is built into the cell by the experimenter, so the molecular actuator decides which cells respond, and the light only has to reach the tissue [90].

### Where the chain breaks

<!-- synthesis -->
Two joins fail more often than the others in this literature. One is between biophysics and molecules: between the physical coupling event and the molecular signalling that is supposed to follow from it. The other is between animal mechanism and human outcome. The claim that these are the two weakest joins is the Atlas's reading across the corpus, not a statement any one source makes [24,69,199].

The first join is weak because the transducing cell and molecule are often unknown. In rodent preparations, Oh and colleagues reported that low-intensity, low-frequency ultrasound was transduced not by neurons but by astrocytes: astrocytic TRPA1 channels opened, the calcium entry drove glutamate release through Best1 channels, and that glutamate activated NMDA receptors on nearby neurons to produce firing [69]. This account is contested. Other work attributes ultrasound transduction to neuronal channels, and one study found that deleting astrocytic Piezo1 had little effect [69]. Until this is settled, the ultrasound arrow from coupling to cellular response has two rival paths (section 5).

The drug side has a related gap between target and downstream step. In male mice, Zanos and colleagues found an inverted-U dose-response for ketamine's antidepressant-like behavioural effect, effective at 10 mg/kg and ineffective at higher doses [168]. Pretreatment with the NMDA receptor antagonists MK-801 or CPP abolished the effect. The same pretreatment also blocked the effects of (2R,6R)-HNK, a GABA-A modulator and an mGlu2/3 antagonist [168]. The authors proposed that structurally unrelated compounds converge on a shared requirement for downstream NMDA receptor activation [168]. That proposal is disputed by accounts that place ketamine's action at opioid or GABAergic sites, or that explain (2R,6R)-HNK differently [168]. A decisive test would show whether the NMDA receptor-dependent step is required for each compound in the same preparation and at the doses used in people.

The second join is between animal mechanism and human outcome. Morgan's 43% figure is a measure of this gap in drug development: in those programmes the human trial could not show whether the mechanism had been engaged at all [24]. For chronic antidepressants, Harmer, Duman and Cowen argue that a neurotransmitter-level account cannot explain the delay before clinical benefit or the reversal of varied symptoms. They set out two middle-level frameworks, early changes in emotional and social information processing and drug-induced plasticity, which place the delay between receptor occupancy and symptom change [187]. Whether the serotonin-deficit premise behind this drug class holds is itself disputed [187].

Circuit-level theories carry much of the weight at this join. Aston-Jones and Cohen, drawing on primate electrophysiology and computational modelling, argue that the locus coeruleus sets neural gain in two modes: phasic responses support task engagement, and elevated tonic firing accompanies disengagement and exploration [138]. Fries proposes that communication between neuronal groups depends on coherence, because rhythmic excitability opens windows in which input can be received [128]. Both accounts are used to explain how a drive reaches behaviour [128,138].

### Nominal and effective stimulus

Bergmann and Hartwigsen lay out the steps from the electric field induced by non-invasive brain stimulation, through local and network effects, to behaviour, and list confounds at each step that weaken a causal claim [199]. Morgan's pillars separate administered dose, exposure at the target, and target engagement for the same reason [24].

<!-- synthesis -->
The Atlas names the distinction these two sources draw. The *nominal stimulus* is what the experimenter set: the device parameters, or the administered dose and route. The *effective stimulus* is what actually acted on the tissue: the field at the neuron, the pressure at the transducing cell, the drug concentration at the receptor, or a co-stimulated sensory or peripheral input that a confound analysis would flag [24,199]. Studies often report the first and infer the second. The distinction recurs in later sections.

### Why a graph forces the choices

Prose can leave much unstated; a graph edge cannot. SCKAN, the SPARC connectivity knowledge base of the autonomic nervous system, records curated origins, terminations and routes of autonomic projections, with an ontological representation and documented provenance drawn from expert input, literature, textbooks and datasets [155]. It is the closest existing model for the Atlas graph.

<!-- synthesis -->
Writing the chain as edges forces four choices. *Direction*: an edge runs from one level to another, so a reflex loop such as Tracey's has to be drawn as a cycle rather than a line [149]. *Sign of effect*: an edge must say whether it raises or lowers its target. Zanos's inverted-U shows that the sign can depend on dose, so the sign belongs on a dose-qualified edge rather than on the drug [168]. Aston-Jones and Cohen's phasic/tonic split shows that it can also depend on regime [138]. *Species*: each edge must record whether it was measured in human, primate, rodent, cell culture or a model, because the joins above are where species change [24,69,90,138]. *Evidence*: each edge must say whether it rests on an experiment, a review or a simulation, and whether a rival edge exists [8,69,155,168]. These choices are the reason the chain is also the graph's spine.

## 3. Dose

<!-- framing -->
This section argues that dose is a set of parameters rather than a single number. It starts with electric and magnetic stimulation, then takes ultrasound, light and drugs in turn, and ends with what a graph schema has to store as a result.

### 3.1 What dose means for electric and magnetic stimulation

The reference definition comes from a 2012 review by Peterchev and colleagues. It starts from the premise that the biological effects of transcranial electric stimulation (tES) and transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) are mediated by the electric field injected into the body (tES) or induced in it (TMS) [9]. On that premise, dose is every device-side parameter that determines the field: the electrode or coil configuration, the waveform, the pulse parameters, and the timing of pulses, trains and sessions [9]. The same review keeps three things apart: the dose the operator controls, the field that results in tissue, and the physiological effect [9]. On this definition, "2 mA" or "120% of motor threshold" is one entry in a parameter set, not the dose itself. Its reporting recommendations follow the principle of reproducibility [9].

Parameters other than amplitude change the effect. In rat locus coeruleus, recorded across a grid of vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) settings, higher current and longer pulse widths increased firing rate. Pulse frequency changed when the phasic burst occurred but not the total amount of locus coeruleus activity. Pulse-locked activity in the trigeminal control nucleus appeared only above 1.2 mA [140]. In humans, a living Bayesian meta-analysis of 18 transcutaneous auricular VNS studies (N = 771) found strong evidence for a pupil effect with pulsed stimulation (g = 0.36, BF10 = 50.8) and strong evidence for the null with continuous stimulation (g = 0.002, BF01 = 21.9) [146].

Timing relative to brain state and to the task can also be part of dose. In eight patients with advanced Parkinson's disease, subthalamic stimulation gated by beta-band local field potentials improved blinded motor scores by 50%. That was about 27% better than continuous stimulation, and total stimulation time fell by 56% [135]. In a meta-analysis of 16 randomised trials of prefrontal TMS or transcranial direct current stimulation in healthy adults, cortisol responses to a laboratory stressor fell more when stimulation came before or after the stressor than during it [159]. An expert classification of implantable electrical therapies for chronic pain proposes classifying them by target and by waveform and dose. It notes that published studies often omit the effective dose, preventing replication [2].

The word "dose" is used in two ways in this literature. In Peterchev's usage it is the device-side specification, and the field in tissue is its consequence [9]. Others use "dose" for the field in tissue itself. A 2024 perspective, for example, proposes calibrating TMS intensity to the modelled cortical electric field (E-field) instead of to the motor threshold [15]. This book follows Peterchev and calls the in-tissue quantity *exposure*. The two usages name different nodes, not rival theories.

### 3.2 E-field modelling as dosimetry, and the missing metric

Because one device setting produces different fields in different heads, finite-element pipelines estimate the field person by person from MRI. SimNIBS was introduced alongside a statement of three barriers to routine TMS field modelling: model uncertainty, linking the calculated field to physiology, and usability [10]. A later implementation was validated against analytical solutions. Its authors found that accurate tissue geometry and a sufficiently accurate numerical method mattered most for reliable results [11]. The CHARM segmentation separates 15 head tissues. Uncorrected imaging artefacts produced local E-field differences above 30%, and the extra tissue classes improved estimates over five-tissue models [12]. ROAST, an independent open-source tES pipeline, was compared with other software and with intracranial recordings in humans, and the reported differences were small [13].

Measurement anchors the models. In 10 epilepsy patients with intracranial electrodes, 2 mA applied at the scalp produced measured cortical fields of about 0.8 V/m. Individual finite-element models correlated with the measurements (r = 0.86 at cortical and r = 0.88 at depth electrodes), and calibrating tissue conductivities improved accuracy [40]. Direct recordings in nonhuman primates and in epilepsy patients put peak tES field strengths at about 0.5 mV/mm in superficial cortex, with little change across frequency or region [211]. In a spherical-head simulation of 50 TMS coils, every coil traded depth against focality [56]. Across species, finite-element models of mouse, monkey and human heads predicted tES fields differing by up to about 100-fold. Animal doses therefore do not transfer to humans unless the fields are matched [212].

<!-- figure: fig3 -->


Modelling a field is not the same as agreeing on what to extract from it. A systematic review of 202 tES and TMS studies found 308 different E-field outcome measures: 197 volume-based and 111 surface-based [14]. The same authors then ran 1,000 models in 100 people across ten montages and coils. The different measures gave different E-field values, sampled different brain regions and did not always correlate strongly [14]. In the preprint version, region-of-interest measures and percentile-based whole-brain measures overlapped by only about 6% on average within a person [14]. The authors state that there is still no consensus on which E-field feature to extract or how to extract it [14]. E-field-based TMS dosing remains a proposal whose proponents list simulation, target definition and cross-region thresholds as open, and motor-threshold dosing remains the clinical norm [15].

<!-- synthesis -->
The result is a gap between two levels. The device-side dose is well defined and often badly reported [2,9]. The exposure at the tissue can be modelled, and has been checked against intracranial recordings in a small number of patients [40,211]. But the step from exposure to a single number has no agreed rule [14]. A graph that stores "E-field at target = x V/m" without naming the metric and the pipeline stores a value that cannot be compared with any other [11,12,14].

### 3.3 Charge and charge density: the safety-limiting quantities

For implanted electrodes, the dose that matters for safety is not the field in the target but the charge passed through the electrode–tissue interface. McCreery and colleagues pulsed platinum surface electrodes of 0.002–0.5 cm² on cat cerebral cortex for 7 h. They used charge-balanced pulse pairs of 400 µs per phase at 50 Hz [16]. Neural injury depended on charge density and charge per phase acting together, across roughly 10–800 µC/cm² and 0.05–5 µC/phase, and neither variable alone predicted damage [16]. Shannon fitted a single model to earlier tissue-damage data, including McCreery's. It gives a log-linear boundary between safe and damaging combinations of the two quantities, with a parameter *k* [17]. This "Shannon equation" became a widely used design rule for macroelectrode stimulation [17].

Whether that rule generalises is disputed (group H-charge-safety). Cogan and colleagues point out that the Shannon equation leaves out frequency, duty cycle, current density and electrode size. They also note that microelectrodes can deliver higher charge densities without damage, and they call for revised thresholds and new data for new bioelectronic devices [18]. One position treats the boundary as a general limit [17]. The other treats it as an empirical fit of limited scope; its best-known source dataset came from cat cortex, platinum electrodes and a single pulse regime [16,18]. Damage data that vary frequency, duty cycle and electrode size independently, in the electrode classes now in use, would settle it [18].

### 3.4 Convulsive therapy: where charge was the dose

In electroconvulsive therapy (ECT), the conventional dosing units are total charge, energy and multiples of the seizure threshold [261]. A randomised trial in 90 depressed patients crossed electrode placement (right unilateral versus bilateral) with pulse width (conventional 1.5 ms versus ultrabrief 0.3 ms) [260]. Ultrabrief right unilateral ECT had the mildest cognitive side effects on virtually every primary measure. It reached 73% remission, against 35% for ultrabrief bilateral treatment [260]. Efficacy and cognitive burden depended jointly on where the current entered and how long each pulse lasted, not on delivered charge alone [260].

Peterchev and colleagues analysed the ECT stimulus parameter by parameter: pulse amplitude, shape and width, and train frequency, directionality, polarity and duration [261]. They argue that each has distinct neurobiological consequences, so total charge and energy are inadequate summaries [261]. They point out that shortening pulse width while raising pulse count had already cut adverse effects without losing efficacy. They conclude that dosage should be defined by the full parameter set, and that devices should expose and display every parameter [261].

<!-- synthesis -->
This is the strongest independent statement of this section's thesis in the corpus. It was made about a clinical therapy with its own dosing tradition, two years before the general tES/TMS definition, and it rejects the summary units that therapy used [9,261].

Train frequency shows why. In four rhesus macaques, seizure threshold was mapped against frequency for ECT (5–240 pulses per second, 230–565 mA) and for magnetic seizure therapy (MST; 5–100 pulses per second, 66–86% of maximum stimulator output) [226]. Mid-range frequencies needed the fewest pulses, and so the least charge and energy. The minimum was near 16 pulses per second, with near-equal thresholds at 10 and 25 pulses per second [226]. Lower frequencies produced more powerful seizures but needed more pulses in total. The relationship was comparable for the electrical and magnetic modes [226]. On the authors' reading, the frequencies used clinically for ECT and MST sit well above this charge-minimising optimum [226]. Spatial distribution differs too. A finite-element simulation comparing four ECT electrode configurations with three MST coils found that MST fields were confined much more to superficial cortex, while ECT stimulated a substantially larger brain volume [225]. The authors propose this focality as the plausible basis for MST's milder cognitive side effects, which the simulation itself does not test [225].

### 3.5 Ultrasound dose

Acoustic dose has more named quantities than electrical dose, and they are not interchangeable. The ITRUSST reporting consensus for low-intensity transcranial ultrasound stimulation (TUS) asks for six domains: the transducer and drive system, the drive settings, the free-field acoustic parameters, the pulse timing, in situ estimates of brain exposure, and the intensity parameters [20]. It separates what the transducer emits into water (free field) from what is estimated to arrive in the brain (in situ). This mirrors Peterchev's split between device dose and tissue field [9,20]. The skull sits between the two. In 25 patients treated with MR-guided focused ultrasound, the skull density ratio correlated positively with peak target temperature (p = 0.009) and skull volume correlated negatively (p = 0.023) [87].

The companion ITRUSST safety consensus covers mechanical and thermal effects separately. For mechanical effects, it classes TUS as non-significant risk when the mechanical index (MI, or the transcranial variant MItc) is 1.9 or lower [19]. For thermal effects, it offers three alternative criteria: temperature rise, absolute temperature, and thermal-dose limits by tissue type [19]. The authors are explicit about the status of these numbers. The document is expert consensus opinion that can inform regulations and standards but not replace them; its values are not safety limits as such; and more data are needed to locate the threshold for significant risk [19]. The reporting document is likewise expert consensus [20]. This book treats neither as a regulatory limit.

Thermal dose has an older and more transferable definition. Sapareto and Dewey converted any time–temperature history into an equivalent exposure time at 43 °C, the quantity now written CEM43 [85]. In the general form

$$\mathrm{CEM43} = \sum_i t_i \, R^{\,(43 - T_i)},$$

$t_i$ is the time spent at temperature $T_i$, and $R$ takes different values above and below a break temperature [85]. The authors presented it as a practical standard, not the only possible one [85]. At high thermal dose the same modality ablates: MR-guided focused ultrasound thalamotomy for essential tremor reduced hand-tremor scores from 18.1 to 9.6 at three months, against 16.0 to 15.8 after sham [86].

Within the low-intensity range, which acoustic parameter carries the effect is itself contested. A systematic review of 32 human TUS studies (37 experiments) scored outcomes as enhancement or suppression. Duty cycle predicted direction (p = 0.046), and so did sonication duration (p = 0.04, shorter favouring excitation). Spatial-peak pulse-average intensity (ISPPA) alone did not (p = 0.256) [79]. The authors call these results tentative and cite incomplete parameter reporting [79]. In rat thalamus, by contrast, ultrasound suppression of evoked potentials did not depend on duty cycle, peak pressure or modulation frequency. It tracked measured temperature change instead [71]. Pulse shape also matters: in mice, smoothing the envelope removed auditory brainstem responses but left motor responses intact [76], and in 25 humans, ramped, longer, lower-intensity pulses reduced scalp sensations for a given dose [78].

When microbubbles are present, dose can be defined by the physical response rather than by the applied energy. In 27 rats, O'Reilly and Hynynen ramped pressure (551.5 kHz, 10 ms bursts, 2 Hz pulse repetition frequency) until ultraharmonic emissions from the microbubbles appeared. They then held pressure at a set fraction of that threshold [81]. Mean peak pressure for successful blood–brain barrier opening was 0.28 ± 0.05 MPa. At 50% scaling the barrier opened with no detectable damage, and higher scaling caused gross damage [81]. The microbubble dose is a separate variable. In rats, clinical imaging doses produced no significant NF-κB pathway change, whereas high doses produced oedema, neuronal degeneration and microhaemorrhage [84]. That finding is contested by work reporting sterile inflammation after opening, taken up in section 14 [84].

### 3.6 Optical dose

For photobiomodulation (PBM), the reported quantities are wavelength, irradiance (mW/cm²) and fluence (J/cm²) [21]. A review by proponents argues that low-level light shows a biphasic dose response, in which low fluence stimulates more than high fluence. It attributes the field's mixed results partly to difficult parameter choice [21]. That claim rests mostly on in vitro and peripheral data [21].

For transcranial PBM, the prior question is how much light arrives (group H-pbm-dose). Tittelmeier and colleagues measured transmission through human skull from commercial transcranial devices. More than 99% of the light was absorbed or scattered. Maximum transmittance was 0.31% for a 905 nm laser and 0.71% for an 810 nm LED helmet [22]. In their cell experiments, mitochondrial stimulation appeared only at intensities 14–775 times above the maximum transmitted level [22]. They conclude that current devices deliver too little light to stimulate cortex directly, so any benefit is more likely indirect or placebo [22]. Against this, Monte Carlo photon-transport simulations at 810 and 1064 nm estimated that up to about 15% of incident energy reached cortex transcranially and about 1% intranasally. Deposited energy scaled linearly with power density and fell substantially with higher skin melanin [23]. The simulation study is a preprint, and one co-author is affiliated with a PBM device company [23]. The two figures are not directly comparable. One is a measured transmittance, the other a modelled fraction of deposited energy [22,23]. Paired measurement and modelling in the same specimens and devices, using one defined quantity, would settle it.

<!-- synthesis -->
The optical case shows that a mechanism demonstrated at the cell does not establish a delivered dose at the target [21,22]. It also adds a covariate, skin pigmentation, that has no counterpart in the electrical reporting standards cited here [9,23].

### 3.7 Pharmacological dose: exposure, not amount

The pharmacological counterpart of Peterchev's split was stated from drug-development failures. In an analysis of 44 Pfizer Phase II programmes, lack of efficacy was the main reason for failure. Yet in 43% of cases it was unclear whether the mechanism had been adequately tested [24]. The authors proposed three "pillars" that must be shown: exposure at the target site, target binding, and expression of pharmacological activity [24]. The milligram amount is the device setting of pharmacology. Exposure, summarised as quantities such as AUC and Cmax, is what exposure–response analysis links to clinical endpoints [25]. Plasma exposure is itself a proxy. At the receptor, what matters is the brain extracellular fluid concentration, set by diffusion, influx and efflux at the blood–brain barrier and distribution among CNS compartments, and usually estimated with physiologically based models tied to animal data [164].

Route and schedule are therefore dose variables. In 20 patients with spinal spasticity who had failed oral baclofen, intrathecal infusion reduced muscle tone in all patients. The molecule was the same; only the compartment changed [163]. In convection-enhanced delivery, volume of distribution scaled linearly with infused volume [160]. Brexanolone is given as a single 60-hour intravenous infusion [180]. Zuranolone, which shares its target pharmacology, is given as 50 mg orally once daily for 14 days [181]. For levodopa, erratic plasma uptake and dose-to-dose variability drive much of the clinical picture [186]. Exposure can also dissociate from effect duration. Intravenous ketamine 0.5 mg/kg in 18 patients with treatment-resistant depression produced improvement within 110 minutes that persisted for about a week [166]. The claim jumps from exposure to a clinical outcome; the trial measured nothing at the levels between, which sections 6 and 7 take up.

Receptor occupancy measured with positron emission tomography (PET) is the closest thing pharmacology has to a mechanism-level dose metric. Twenty-two first-episode patients with schizophrenia were randomised to haloperidol 1.0 or 2.5 mg/day. After two weeks, [11C]raclopride PET showed D2 occupancy between 38% and 87% [26]. The likelihood of clinical response, extrapyramidal symptoms and hyperprolactinaemia rose above about 65%, 72% and 78% occupancy respectively [26]. A later review gives 65–80% D2 occupancy as the antipsychotic therapeutic window and 70–80% serotonin transporter (SERT) occupancy for antidepressants [27].

The threshold model breaks when intrinsic efficacy varies (group H-d2-occupancy). A dose–response meta-analysis of 110 studies (37,193 participants) found that extrapyramidal risk rose steeply above roughly 75–85% D2 occupancy for antagonists. Partial agonists such as aripiprazole kept that risk low despite high occupancy [28]. One position treats an occupancy window as a transferable dose metric [26,27]. The other says occupancy predicts effect only once the ligand's intrinsic efficacy is specified [28]. Occupancy–outcome data across ligands of graded efficacy at the same receptor would decide how far the first position holds. Occupancy also presumes that the administered compound is the acting ligand. For chemogenetics, PET and pharmacology showed that systemically injected clozapine N-oxide does not enter the brain and has low affinity for designer receptors (DREADDs). It is converted in vivo to clozapine, which enters the brain and occupies them [29]. The dose was the exposure to a metabolite [29].

## 4. Coupling I: electric and magnetic fields meet membranes

<!-- framing -->
This section asks what an applied electric field does to a neuron. It starts with the physics of extracellular stimulation, then takes uniform-field polarization, the field actually delivered to a human brain, what TMS activates, the entrainment dispute and temporal interference in turn, and ends with what the coupling level requires of a graph schema.

### 4.1 The physics of extracellular stimulation

An extracellular electrode does not inject current into a neuron; it imposes a potential on the tissue around it, and the neuron responds to how that potential varies in space. Rattay modelled an axon as a chain of discrete compartments — nodes of Ranvier for myelinated fibres, arbitrary segments for unmyelinated ones — combining the time-dependent membrane equation of each compartment with spatial difference equations for the extracellular potential [33]. Both fibre types obey the same formal structure, and the term that drives excitation is the second spatial difference of the extracellular potential along the fibre [33]:

$$f \;\propto\; \frac{\partial^{2} V_{e}}{\partial x^{2}}.$$

This quantity is the *activating function*. Where it is positive the membrane depolarises, where it is negative it hyperpolarises, and the pattern predicted for a monopolar electrode generalises to other electrode layouts [33]. The result is theoretical: it is a property of a cable model, not a measurement [33].

The consequence is geometric. A straight fibre lying in a spatially uniform field has no second derivative of potential along its length and no driving term, so polarisation is concentrated where the geometry breaks — at fibre terminations, at bends, and at points where the field gradient changes [33]. The same logic sets the order in which elements are recruited. Ranck's review of focal electrical stimulation of the mammalian CNS compiled current–distance relations for myelinated fibres and for cell bodies near monopolar electrodes and found that axons generally have lower thresholds than somata, while equivalent data for other electrode types were lacking [34]. Myelinated fibres in that literature usually required less cathodal than anodal current, and fibre orientation relative to current flow mattered [34]. Strong cathodal currents also block propagation, so that only a shell of elements around the electrode is effectively excited [34].

Pulse duration selects among elements as well. Chronaxie — the pulse width at which threshold is twice the value reached with an infinitely long pulse — is about 50–100 µs for central myelinated axons, whereas some grey-matter stimulation gives 200–700 µs, which Ranck took as leaving open what is being excited in grey matter [34]. Fibre diameter orders recruitment in the same way. A hybrid finite-element and biophysical model of lumbosacral epidural stimulation predicted that large myelinated dorsal-root afferents are recruited first, not motoneurons or interneurons directly, and that motor pools are then engaged through synapses; the model's predictions about intensity and electrode position were confirmed in rats [61]. Recruitment order is therefore the reverse of physiological recruitment: the largest fibres go first.

The same cable physics also blocks. Modelling of an optimised, charge-balanced 10 kHz waveform showed that sodium channels can be driven into inactivation by the closed-state path, producing conduction block without the onset burst that conventional kilohertz block causes; the work is computational [62]. At the electrode itself the constraint is not the field but charge transfer across the electrode–tissue interface, which may be Faradaic or non-Faradaic and which sets the limits on charge balance and electrode material [63]. Shannon's model relates charge per phase and charge density to safe versus damaging stimulation along a log-linear boundary [17]; section 3 treats the dispute over that rule's scope.

### 4.2 Uniform fields: measured polarization and its orientation dependence

For transcranial methods the field across any single neuron is close to uniform, so the relevant measurement is how much membrane polarisation a given field produces. In rat hippocampal slices, uniform DC fields below 40 mV/mm (40 V/m) applied parallel to the somato-dendritic axis polarised CA1 pyramidal somata linearly, at a mean of about 0.12 mV per mV/mm [36]. Polarisation varied along the cell and was largest at the dendritic tips, with time constants of about 15–70 ms [36]. The two ends of a neuron polarise with opposite sign: the field that depolarises the soma hyperpolarises the distal dendrite [36]. Stronger fields triggered spontaneous firing and epileptiform activity, and excitability changes outlasted the field by more than a second [36].

Orientation is not a modifier of that coupling constant but a condition of it. Fields applied perpendicular to the somato-dendritic axis did not polarise the soma at all, yet still changed afferent synaptic responses [36]. The authors concluded that uniform fields modulate excitability continuously through compartment-specific polarisation, with no clear threshold [36]. Morphology sets the coupling as well. Patch-clamp recordings from reconstructed rat motor-cortex neurons in uniform fields showed that morphology predicts somatic subthreshold polarisation, with layer V pyramidal cells the most sensitive; suprathreshold thresholds reflected both direct polarisation and network input and were lower in layers V/VI than in II/III [49]. Field stimulation in that preparation produced burst firing that somatic current injection did not [49].

<!-- synthesis -->
Two mechanisms therefore sit under the same word "polarization" and do not scale together: somatic polarisation, which is proportional to field magnitude along the somato-dendritic axis and has been measured in rodent slices [36,49], and terminal or bend polarisation, which the cable analysis predicts dominates in a uniform field because that is where the second spatial derivative of potential is non-zero [33]. A claim about somata does not license a claim about terminals, and the two predict different sensitivities to electrode or coil orientation [33,36].

The threshold for modulating ongoing activity is far below the threshold for initiating it. Terzuolo and Bullock set out to quantify how sensitive nerve cells are to an imposed voltage gradient and found, in invertebrate neurons, that changing the firing frequency of an already-active cell requires a much smaller gradient than exciting a silent one [35]. Network state can amplify further. In ferret cortex, endogenous fields during physiological activity reached about 2–4 mV/mm, and in ferret cortical slices applied fields of that same amplitude sped up, regularised and entrained the slow oscillation; closed-loop feedback proportional to ongoing activity strengthened oscillatory structure under positive feedback and weakened it under negative feedback [37]. The attribution of the large network effect to small, simultaneous depolarisation of many neurons came from network modelling rather than from the recordings [37].

### 4.3 How much field arrives in a human brain

The numbers above are the denominator; the delivered field is the numerator. In 10 epilepsy patients with intracranial electrodes, potentials recorded during scalp stimulation showed that 2 mA at the scalp produced measured cortical fields of about 0.8 V/m, lower than commonly assumed [40]. Individual finite-element head models correlated well with the measurements (r = 0.86 for cortical and r = 0.88 for depth electrodes); calibrating tissue conductivities improved accuracy, while adding white-matter anisotropy or a multi-layer skull did not [40]. Most of the applied current never reaches the brain: about 75% of scalp-applied current is shunted by soft tissue and skull [50]. A modelling study of morphologically realistic layer-5 pyramidal cells put the field available at tolerable scalp currents at about 0.1–1 V/m in the human brain [48].

Against that, intracellular recordings in rats placed the minimum field for affecting spiking and subthreshold activity at about 1 mV/mm (1 V/m), and in living humans about 4–6 mA was needed to modulate EEG alpha reliably [50]. Coil geometry sets the corresponding limits for magnetic stimulation. Finite-element simulations of 50 TMS coils in a spherical head model found a depth–focality tradeoff in every coil, with tangential spreads as small as 5 cm² for figure-8 types against 34 cm² for circular types over a similar half-value depth range of 0.9–3.5 cm; all those values are simulated [56]. The safety envelope is wider than the efficacious one is certain: pooled evidence from more than 300,000 low-intensity tES sessions between 2017 and 2025 reported no serious tES-related adverse events and supports use up to 4 mA for up to 60 min/day [43], and the IFCN-endorsed TMS update concludes that seizure risk remains low with conventional parameters [42].

### 4.4 What TMS activates

The consensus position on TMS is a statement about geometry, not intensity. The induced time-varying field acts mainly on superficial gyral cortex because it penetrates poorly with depth, and the most likely primary targets are myelinated axon terminals of pyramidal cells and interneurons at the gyral crown; activity then spreads through local intracortical circuits and along white-matter projections in a state-dependent way [41]. The consensus separates measured from modelled evidence and notes that TMS also produces substantial somatosensory and auditory co-stimulation requiring control [41]. The modelling that supports it coupled realistic neuron morphologies to TMS fields in a head model and predicted that the lowest thresholds are in intracortical axon terminals at the gyral crown and lip, with layer-5 pyramidal cells the most excitable cells; field magnitude mattered more than direction, and changing current direction shifted activation sites in a waveform-dependent way [57]. Those findings are computational throughout [57].

Human epidural recordings of descending corticospinal volleys give the measured counterpart: D- and I-wave patterns show how TMS, transcranial electrical stimulation and tDCS parameters, including intensity and current direction, selectively engage different excitatory and inhibitory circuits, with large inter-individual variability [58]. Which circuit a pulse recruits also predicts who responds to patterned protocols. Theta-burst stimulation, bursts of high-frequency pulses repeated at theta rhythm, was introduced as a protocol in which 20–190 s over human motor cortex produced lasting excitability changes whose direction depended on whether the train was continuous or intermittent [38]. In 56 healthy adults, theta-burst after-effects varied widely, and the variation correlated with the latency of motor evoked potentials elicited by anterior–posterior current, a marker of late I-wave recruitment; the authors attribute responder status to which interneuron networks a pulse recruits rather than to plasticity capacity [59]. The reproducibility of the original after-effects is therefore disputed between these two reports [38,59]; section 6 takes up the cellular reading and section 12 the variability.

### 4.5 Does 1–2 mA entrain neurons?

<!-- framing -->
The two positions are set out in turn, then what would decide between them.

For direct entrainment: in awake macaques, scalp tACS altered the timing but not the rate of single-neuron spiking, including in deep structures such as hippocampus and basal ganglia [51]. Neocortical single-unit recordings in awake monkeys showed dose-dependent entrainment at intensities feasible in humans, with two response patterns — increased burst firing and phase locking to the stimulus [52]. Large-scale network simulations with supporting ferret recordings add a mechanism: frequency-specific enhancement through network resonance, largest when the stimulation frequency matches the network's intrinsic rhythm, with the hyperpolarising phase delaying premature firing and the depolarising phase synchronising population firing [53]. Entrainment is not uniform across cells. In awake macaques, tACS entrained neurons weakly locked to ongoing oscillations but often reduced phase locking in neurons already strongly locked to an endogenous rhythm, a competition the authors offer as a source of inconsistent human outcomes [44].

Against direct entrainment: the rat threshold of about 1 V/m sits at or above the roughly 0.8 V/m measured intracranially in humans at 2 mA, and the same authors needed about 4–6 mA in living humans to modulate EEG alpha, concluding that conventional low-intensity tES is probably too weak for direct, immediate effects [40,50]. The alternative route is peripheral. In rats and humans, cortical tACS fields were argued to be too weak for direct activation while skin fields are much stronger; anaesthetising the skin reduced tACS-induced tremor entrainment, and peripheral nerve stimulation alone reproduced the motor effects [54]. The rebuttal is a lesion-style control in the same species as the positive evidence: in macaque hippocampus and visual cortex, neurons stayed entrained to tACS after topical anaesthesia blocked somatosensory input [55].

<!-- synthesis -->
What would settle it is a matched comparison rather than more of either kind of study: simultaneous intracranial recording and field measurement in humans at conventional 1–2 mA, with peripheral block applied in the same session, so that delivered field, entrainment and peripheral contribution are measured together in one species at one dose [40,50,54,55]. At present the positive single-unit evidence is in macaques [51,52,55], the sceptical threshold is from rat intracellular recording plus human EEG [50], and the two are joined only by field models [48].

### 4.6 Temporal interference

Temporal interference (TI) proposes to move the coupling site rather than raise the dose. Two kilohertz-range fields at slightly different frequencies are each too fast to drive neurons, but where they overlap they produce a low-frequency amplitude envelope that can; the proposal was validated in modelling and physical field measurements, and in anaesthetised mice neurons followed the envelope, with hippocampal stimulation achieved without driving the overlying cortex and motor responses steered by changing the current ratio [39]. Both the mechanism and the human scaling are disputed. Modelling shows that passive membrane filtering cannot extract the envelope, because the waveform's content is entirely high-frequency, while ion-channel rectification can; the same model predicts tonic firing or conduction block in superficial tissue at intensities that activate deep targets, a "sandwich" problem for the focality claim [60].

The threshold numbers are modelled, and they are large. In morphologically realistic layer-5 pyramidal cell models, suprathreshold TI required about 75–230 V/m, similar to kilohertz tACS and far above low-frequency tACS at about 17–47 V/m, with conduction block above about 1,700 V/m; set against the roughly 0.1–1 V/m available at tolerable scalp currents, the authors conclude that suprathreshold human TI is infeasible without anaesthesia, and that three-dimensional morphology matters in a way earlier one-dimensional analyses missed [48]. None of those thresholds is a measurement [48]. Human work reports effects at subthreshold amplitudes instead: modelling and cadaver measurements showed TI fields can be steered to the hippocampus with less exposure of overlying cortex, and in healthy volunteers TI changed hippocampal fMRI activity during memory encoding and improved episodic-memory accuracy [45]. The focality there is modelled and cadaver-derived; the neural and behavioural effects were measured in living participants [45].

The primate comparison is the most direct test of potency. In macaques, TI-tACS changed spike timing but not firing rate, and the requirements of TI — high carrier frequencies, multiple electrode pairs, amplitude modulation — together made it roughly 80% less potent than conventional tACS, which led the authors to propose TI as a way of disrupting pathological rhythms rather than imposing new ones [46]. A systematic review searching to December 2024 found 48 records — 20 completed human studies and 28 ongoing trials, 820 participants in total — with good tolerability, reported engagement of motor cortex, basal ganglia and hippocampus mostly at 20 Hz or gamma-range envelopes, and small single-session reports of improved Parkinsonian tremor and bradykinesia; the authors classify the evidence as Phase 1 level, with efficacy untested [47].

### 4.7 What the coupling level requires of a graph

<!-- synthesis -->
Coupling is a function of geometry and fibre type, so "stimulation" is not an edge. Four properties have to travel with it. First, orientation: the field component parallel to the somato-dendritic axis, because perpendicular fields do not polarise the soma [36], and because current direction shifts the modelled activation site for TMS [57] and changes which circuits a human motor-cortex pulse recruits [58]. Second, the neural element and its class: myelinated axon versus soma [34], axon terminal at a gyral crown [41,57], large-diameter dorsal-root afferent [61], layer V versus layer II/III pyramidal cell [49]. Third, field magnitude with its unit, its species and its provenance — measured intracranially in humans [40], intracellularly in rats [50], or modelled [48,56]. Fourth, whether the claim is that the field initiated activity or modulated ongoing activity, a distinction with its own threshold asymmetry [35] and its own state dependence [44].

<!-- synthesis -->
Two of those edges must also be able to carry rivals rather than a single mechanism: the route of low-intensity tES effects, cortical or peripheral [51,52,54,55], and the mechanism and feasibility of temporal interference, envelope demodulation by rectification at fields no human montage delivers [39,48,60] against measured subthreshold human effects [45] and reduced potency in macaques [46].

## 5. Coupling II: pressure, light, heat and ligands

<!-- framing -->
Section 4 asked what an electric field does to a membrane. This section asks the same of the four remaining non-electrical drives: pressure, light, heat and ligands. Each has a candidate primary transducer, and in each case a rival account explains the same observations. The section takes the rivals in turn rather than choosing between them.

### 5.1 What a pressure wave delivers to tissue

An acoustic wave does several things to tissue at once, and the field disagrees about which carries the neural effect. Momentum transfer produces acoustic radiation force and micron-scale tissue displacement, imaged directly in isolated salamander retina during sonication [66]. Absorption produces heating, and any heating history reduces to an equivalent time at a reference temperature, written CEM43 [85]. Where gas bodies are present the wave drives bubble oscillation instead of being absorbed, which is why microbubble techniques work far below ablative intensities [80]. In the near field at the scalp, a preregistered study in 25 human participants concluded that particle displacement rather than radiation force drives the tactile, thermal and painful sensations ultrasound produces [78].

The skull sits between the transducer and all of this. In 25 human patients treated with MR-guided focused ultrasound, skull density ratio correlated positively with peak achieved target temperature (p = 0.009) and skull volume correlated negatively (p = 0.023) [87]. Skull anatomy bounds the deliverable dose, and skull density ratio became a patient-selection criterion [87].

### 5.2 Four accounts of low-intensity ultrasound, and the confound that competes with all of them

The first account is membrane-intrinsic. The neuronal intramembrane cavitation excitation (NICE) model treats the lipid bilayer as a bilayer sonophore whose leaflets separate and re-approach under an acoustic field, rectifying the wave into a periodic capacitance change that drives displacement currents and, through Hodgkin–Huxley-type conductances, spiking [65]. This is a computational model, not a measurement: it reproduced previously published thresholds across pulsing regimes and predicted that duty cycle and intensity should bias excitation against suppression [65]. Bilayer mechanics alone suffice, with no dedicated receptor [65].

The second account names proteins. In *Xenopus* oocytes expressing candidate mechanosensitive channels, focused ultrasound at 10 MHz and 0.3–4.9 W/cm² modulated TREK-1, TREK-2, TRAAK and NaV1.5 currents reversibly by up to about 23% on average, abolished by ranolazine for NaV1.5 and barium for the two-pore-domain channels [67]. The loss-of-function evidence in an intact animal is from mouse: conditional Piezo1 knockout in motor cortex sharply reduced ultrasound-evoked calcium responses, limb movement and EMG, ex vivo and in vivo, and central amygdala, which expresses more Piezo1, was more ultrasound-sensitive than motor cortex [68]. A competing cellular assignment puts the transducer in glia: in rodent, low-intensity low-frequency ultrasound was reported to open astrocytic TRPA1, with calcium entry driving glutamate release through Best1 channels and glutamate then activating NMDA receptors on neighbouring neurons [69]. The two are in tension, because neuronal but not astrocytic Piezo1 deletion cut the amygdala response in the Piezo1 study [68,69]. Nor is the repertoire confined to cation channels: CFTR in HEK293T cells passed outward currents under 0.8 MHz, 0.20 MPa ultrasound, abolished by GlyH-101 [70].

The third account replaces the channel question with a question about which physical force arrives. In ex vivo salamander retina, sweeping the carrier frequency from 0.5 to 43 MHz made ganglion-cell responses stronger at higher frequencies, the opposite of what cavitation-based mechanisms predict, which excludes cavitation in that preparation; a quantitative radiation-force model accounted for the responses, and transducer-to-array distance mattered strongly, so standing waves shaped the effective stimulus [66].

The fourth account is thermal. In rat ventral posterolateral thalamus, suppression of tibial-nerve somatosensory evoked potentials scaled with spatial position and intensity but was independent of duty cycle, peak pressure and modulation frequency, tracking measured temperature instead, with no histological damage; heating delivered separately by an optical fibre reproduced the suppression [71]. The parameters a mechanical model says should matter did not [65,71].

Behind all four accounts stands a competing explanation of the results themselves. In guinea pig, transcranial ultrasound produced widespread cortical and subcortical activation that was abolished by transecting the auditory nerves or removing cochlear fluid, so it ascended through the auditory system rather than arising at the focus [74]. In mouse, wide-field calcium imaging found the evoked pattern matched auditory pathway activation rather than the focus, resembled the response to audible sound, and was reduced by chemical deafening [75]. The rebuttal locates the culprit in the envelope: in normal-hearing and genetically deaf mice, rectangular pulses with sharp rising edges activated the auditory pathway, but smoothing the envelope removed the auditory response while leaving the motor response intact, and deaf mice responded identically to hearing mice [76]. In humans the confound reasserts itself. Four experiments at three institutions, one preregistered, indexed corticospinal excitability with TMS-elicited motor evoked potentials during ultrasonic stimulation pulsed at 1000 Hz; the reported motor inhibition replicated, but manipulating intensity, duration and auditory masking showed it was driven by peripheral auditory stimulation, and the authors argue prior work using only flip-over sham must be reinterpreted [77].

<!-- synthesis -->
The four families are not mutually exclusive and are not resolved. Each has its strongest evidence in a different preparation: a model for intramembrane cavitation [65], heterologous expression and knockout for channels [67,68], ex vivo retina for radiation force [66], rat thalamus for heating [71]. The corpus for this volume contains no study identifying the transducer in human tissue. The discriminating tests are partly specified by the papers themselves: a frequency sweep, because cavitation and radiation force predict opposite frequency dependence [66]; duty cycle varied at matched temperature [65,71]; and channel-specific loss of function where the field physics is also measured [68]. Until confound controls are standard, human parameter–outcome aggregation cannot settle it [77,79].


### 5.3 Cavitation with microbubbles: a different target

Sonicating 18 rabbit brains at four to six locations each, at temporal peak acoustic powers of 0.2 to 11.5 W with an ultrasound contrast agent injected before each exposure, opened the blood–brain barrier at the targeted locations; the lowest powers opened it without damaging neurons, and contrast-enhanced MR imaging tracked the opening [80]. The active agent is microbubble oscillation in the vasculature, so the coupling target is the endothelium rather than the neuron [80].

Because the bubble radiates its own acoustic signature, dose can be defined by a measured response instead of applied energy. Using a wideband hydrophone across 86 locations in 27 rats at 551.5 kHz with 10 ms bursts at 2 Hz pulse repetition frequency, pressure was ramped until ultraharmonic emissions appeared, then held at a set fraction of that threshold; mean peak pressure for successful opening was 0.28 ± 0.05 MPa, and at 50% scaling the barrier opened with no detectable damage while higher scaling caused gross damage [81]. In humans, a phase I trial in five patients with early-to-moderate Alzheimer's disease opened the barrier safely, reversibly and repeatedly with no serious clinical or radiographic adverse events at three months, and exploratory florbetaben PET showed no group-level change in amyloid burden [82]. The engineering claim and the therapeutic claim separate there: the barrier opens, and nothing measured changed [82].

Whether the opening is benign is disputed. In rat, pulsed focused ultrasound with systemic microbubbles produced immediate damage-associated molecular patterns and a parenchymal sterile inflammatory response lasting about 24 hours, with a profile the authors compared to ischaemia or mild traumatic brain injury [83]. Against this, a rat study comparing three sonication protocols at different microbubble doses found the dose determined the response: at clinical imaging doses there was no significant change in NF-κB pathway gene expression at 6 hours or 4 days, whereas high doses activated the pathway and produced oedema, neuronal degeneration, neutrophil infiltration and microhaemorrhage [84]. The dispute is unresolved. One position holds that barrier opening is itself a wounding event [83]; the other that inflammation is a function of microbubble dose and exposure, and can be engineered away [84]. Varying microbubble dose and barrier permeability independently, with shared inflammatory read-outs, would decide it [83,84].

### 5.4 Thermal coupling

Heat is a coupling route in its own right, and the only drive here with a transferable dose unit: Sapareto and Dewey converted an arbitrary time–temperature history into an equivalent exposure time at 43 °C, presented as a practical standard rather than the only possible one [85]. At high thermal dose the same acoustic modality ablates. In a randomised sham-controlled trial, MRI-guided focused ultrasound thalamotomy reduced hand-tremor scores in human patients from 18.1 to 9.6 at three months against 16.0 to 15.8 after sham, with gait disturbance in 36% and paraesthesias or numbness in 38% of treated patients [86]. Dose, not modality, decides whether acoustic coupling is reversible modulation or a lesion [85,86].

The contested claim is that the low end of the range is thermal too. The rat thalamic work argues that reversible neuroinhibition tracks measured temperature rather than the mechanical parameters the cavitation account predicts [71]. Those predictions — duty cycle and intensity as directionality knobs — come from NICE [65]. A systematic review of 32 human transcranial ultrasound studies (37 experiments), scoring outcomes as enhancement or suppression, found duty cycle predicted direction (p = 0.046) and sonication duration did too (p = 0.04, shorter favouring excitation), while spatial-peak pulse-average intensity alone did not (p = 0.256); the authors cite selection bias and incomplete parameter reporting [79]. The thermal account therefore rests on a parameter dissociation in rat, the mechanical account on a parameter association in humans that its own authors call labile [71,79]. Neither reports the other's key quantity: the human studies largely do not measure temperature, and the rat study did not run the human protocols [71,79].

### 5.5 Optical coupling

For light the identified transducer is an enzyme. Competing 670 nm LED light against potassium cyanide in primary neuron cultures, Wong-Riley and colleagues found light partially restored cytochrome c oxidase activity blocked by 10–100 µM cyanide and cut cell death from 83.6% to 43.5% at 300 µM, while protection failed at millimolar concentrations [88]. The identification rests on an action spectrum: across 670, 728, 770, 830 and 880 nm, efficacy tracked the absorption spectrum of oxidised cytochrome c oxidase, with 830 and 670 nm effective and 728 nm not [88]. The step from absorption to signalling is attributed to nitric oxide: in HeLa cells irradiated at 600–860 nm, the action spectrum for cell attachment matched the same photoacceptor, and nitric oxide donors given before irradiation shifted it [89]. Both are in vitro results [88,89].

Optical dose is reported as wavelength, irradiance in mW/cm² and fluence in J/cm², and a review by proponents argues the response is biphasic, low fluence stimulating more than high; it attributes the field's mixed results partly to parameter breadth and concedes poor mechanistic understanding [21]. The biphasic claim rests mostly on in vitro and peripheral data [21].

The challenge to transcranial photobiomodulation is not to the mechanism but to the delivered dose. Measuring transmission of near-infrared light from commercial devices through human skull, Tittelmeier and colleagues found more than 99% absorbed or scattered, with maximum transmittance 0.31% for a 905 nm laser and 0.71% for an 810 nm LED helmet; in their cell experiments mitochondrial stimulation appeared only at intensities 14–775 times above the maximum transmitted level, and they conclude any benefit must be indirect or placebo [22]. That measurement is contested by a photon-transport modelling estimate, which section 3 sets out with its citation [22]. It is the volume's cleanest case of a mechanism established at the cell failing to establish a coupling event at the target [22,88].

### 5.6 The contrast case: transducers that were installed

<!-- framing -->
Every dispute above concerns which endogenous structure absorbs the drive. The genetically actuated modalities remove that question by construction.

Delivering channelrhodopsin-2, a light-gated cation channel from algae, to mammalian neurons by lentivirus made brief light pulses drive millisecond-timescale control of spiking and of synaptic transmission, with a single gene and no exogenous cofactor [90]. Specificity comes from the expressed actuator; the physical stimulus only has to arrive [90]. The acoustic analogue followed the same template: wild-type *Caenorhabditis elegans* is insensitive to low-pressure ultrasound, gas-filled microbubbles are needed to transduce the wave, and misexpressing the mechanotransduction subunit TRP-4 in chosen neurons made exactly those neurons ultrasound-responsive [72]. In mammalian cells human TRPA1 served as the sensitising actuator, with gating requiring its N-terminal tip region, cholesterol interactions and an intact actin cytoskeleton; unilateral expression in mouse layer V motor cortex produced c-fos induction and contralateral limb movement to ultrasound delivered through intact skull [73].

<!-- synthesis -->
The installed transducer converts a mechanism question into a delivery question. Because the actuator's identity, kinetics and cell-type distribution are known by construction, a negative result implicates delivery rather than mechanism, and a positive result cannot be attributed to a rival transducer in the same cells [72,73,90]. That is the inference the endogenous cases cannot make [65,66,71]. The reverse lesson also holds: hsTRPA1 gating depended on lipid environment and cytoskeletal tension, so even an installed channel's sensitivity belongs to its cellular context rather than to the pore [73].

### 5.7 Chemical coupling: binding, occupancy, efficacy and compartment

For drugs the coupling step is receptor binding, and pharmacology supplies a worked vocabulary: affinity and occupancy, the operational model for functional systems, the extended ternary complex model for simulating G-protein-coupled receptor behaviour, and allosteric and ligand-selective active-state mechanisms [91]. Two consequences matter here. Occupancy alone does not fix the effect, because intrinsic efficacy and agonist-selective active states intervene between binding and response [91]. And a drive can be a change in a transfer function rather than an input: pregnane steroids enhance GABA-A receptor function by a direct, non-genomic allosteric action, and act both as circulating messengers and as locally synthesised neurosteroids, so one molecule can be a systemic or a local drive [179].

Where the receptor is engaged is part of the coupling, not a pharmacokinetic detail. In rodent and in vitro work, the cortical plasticity-promoting effects of psychedelics were attributed to intracellular rather than cell-surface 5-HT2A receptors, which accounts for why serotonin itself, being membrane-impermeant, does not engage the same mechanism despite being a 5-HT2A agonist [92]. Location at a coarser scale matters too: the reference review of psychedelic pharmacology weights 5-HT2A receptors on the apical dendrites of layer V neocortical pyramidal cells [172]. Nor need the coupling step be a lock-and-key event: a current review of general anaesthesia describes action both inside ion channels and within the plasma membrane, where ordered lipid domains create anaesthetic-sensitive sites, which is the pharmacological parallel of the membrane-level accounts of acoustic coupling [65,184].

The chemogenetic case shows what happens when the acting ligand is assumed rather than measured. Directed evolution of muscarinic receptors produced designer receptors activated by the supposedly inert compound clozapine-N-oxide and not by acetylcholine; a Gi-coupled member expressed in hippocampal neurons hyperpolarised them and silenced firing [93]. Using PET and pharmacology in rodent and rhesus macaque, Gomez and colleagues then showed that systemically injected clozapine-N-oxide does not enter the brain and has low affinity for the designer receptors; it is converted in vivo to clozapine, which enters the brain and occupies them, and subthreshold clozapine doses preferentially produced the designer-receptor behaviour [29]. The nominal ligand was not the acting one [29].

### 5.8 Magnetic actuation and the order-of-magnitude test

Magneto, a single-component actuator made by fusing the cation channel TRPV4 to the paramagnetic protein ferritin, was reported to give non-invasive magnetic control of neuronal activity across calcium imaging, brain-slice and in vivo electrophysiology, and behaviour in zebrafish and mouse [95]. A first-principles analysis then showed that the forces, torques and heating required for ferritin-based magnetic gating exceed what paramagnetic ferritin can supply by roughly five to ten orders of magnitude, so if the reported phenomena are real they must have another cause; magnetothermal nanoparticle heating is not subject to that objection [94]. The claim must be carried as contested. The dispute is asymmetric in kind: one side reports measured effects [95], the other a calculation that no version of the proposed mechanism can produce them [94]. Identifying the actual coupling route in preparations that show the effect, or replicating with the ferritin fusion removed, would settle it [94,95].

### 5.9 What this level implies for a graph

<!-- synthesis -->
Three things follow for a schema. First, the transducer is a node rather than an edge label: cytochrome c oxidase [88], Piezo1 [68], astrocytic TRPA1 [69], CFTR [70], the bilayer itself [65], the microbubble [80], the receptor [91] and the installed actuator [73,90] are entities carrying a cell type, a subcellular compartment and a species of evidence. Receptor compartment must be an attribute, because the same agonist at the same receptor acts differently inside the cell and at the surface [92]. Second, rival transducers are rival edges between the same two nodes, each with its own preparation and discriminating test — a frequency sweep for cavitation against radiation force [66], temperature against duty cycle for thermal against mechanical [65,71], a feasibility calculation for magnetogenetics [94,95]. Third, nominal and effective stimulus must be separable nodes. The wave aimed at cortex and the auditory transient it also produces are different stimuli with different transducers [74,77]; incident and cortical light differ by more than two orders of magnitude [22]; and the compound injected is not always the molecule that binds [29]. A graph storing only the operator's setting cannot represent these disputes.

## 6. What cells do about it

<!-- framing -->
This section takes the cellular level of the chain: the plasticity vocabulary the stimulation field borrowed, the human protocols built on it, the pharmacological and state-dependence evidence that supports the borrowing, the variability literature that limits it, the non-neuronal cells, and what all of this forces a graph to store.

### 6.1 The borrowed vocabulary, and the preparations it came from

Long-term potentiation was described in anaesthetized rabbits. Bliss and Lømo stimulated the perforant path and recorded dentate field responses; brief tetanic stimulation enlarged the population EPSP and shortened its latency, and the enhancement lasted from hours to days [96]. They proposed it as a candidate substrate for memory [96]. The claim that matters here is narrower: a patterned electrical input, delivered for seconds, left synaptic transmission changed long after the input stopped [96]. Every later use of "LTP-like" points back to this result, obtained in an anaesthetized rabbit with an electrode on an identified fibre tract [96].

The sign of a synaptic change was given a rule by Bienenstock, Cooper and Munro. In their formulation the size and direction of a modification depend on current postsynaptic activity relative to a threshold set by the neuron's time-averaged activity; input patterns compete rather than converging fibres [97]. This is a mathematical learning rule whose supporting evidence was that simulations reproduced orientation tuning and ocular-dominance patterns matching data from cats and monkeys reared normally and in altered visual environments [97]. The sliding threshold is a theoretical construct fitted to developmental data, not a quantity measured at a synapse [97].

Abraham and Bear named the general case metaplasticity: earlier activity changes a synapse's capacity for later LTP or LTD without necessarily changing its present strength [98]. They suggested changes in NMDA receptors could account for some instances while other mechanisms probably contribute [98]. Metaplasticity is a change in a threshold, which is a different kind of node from a change in a weight [98].

Homeostatic scaling came from dissociated culture. Turrigiano and colleagues manipulated overall activity in cultured rat neocortical neurons: blocking activity increased quantal (mEPSC) amplitude across all synapses, while blocking GABA-A receptors first raised firing and then let it return toward baseline as amplitudes shrank [99]. Because the adjustment was multiplicative, relative weights were preserved while firing rate was stabilised [99]. That mechanism is separate from Hebbian plasticity, and is the cellular reference for any claim that a stimulation effect is bounded or reversed by compensation [99].

<!-- synthesis -->
Four constructs, four preparations: in vivo rabbit hippocampus, a simulation fitted to visual development, a review-level abstraction, and rat cortical culture [96–99]. None was established in an intact awake human, and none was established with a non-invasive drive. The field's cellular vocabulary is therefore imported across both a species gap and a delivery gap before it is applied to anything measured on a human scalp [96–99].

### 6.2 The human analogues and what "LTP-like" claims

Paired associative stimulation was the first human protocol written directly on a Hebbian timing rule. Stefan and colleagues paired median-nerve stimulation with TMS over motor cortex at a fixed interval in healthy volunteers; motor evoked potential (MEP) amplitudes and silent periods in the target muscle increased within 30 min, lasted at least 30–60 min, and were specific to the targeted representation, which the authors interpreted as associative, LTP-like plasticity [100].

Theta-burst stimulation compressed the exposure. Huang and colleagues applied bursts of high-frequency TMS pulses repeated at a theta rhythm over human motor cortex; 20–190 s changed motor cortical excitability lastingly, with the direction depending on whether the pattern was continuous or intermittent, where earlier repetitive TMS protocols had rarely outlasted about 30 minutes [38]. For transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), the human after-effect is read as polarity-specific: anodal stimulation raises and cathodal stimulation lowers motor cortical excitability [101]. Slice work supports a weaker claim than induction. In rat hippocampal slices, direct current applied during LTP or LTD induction changed the size of the resulting plasticity depending on polarity and dendritic compartment, and required concurrent synaptic activity and NMDA receptors; the authors concluded that direct current modulates plasticity rather than inducing it [106].

One study closes part of the preparation gap from the other end. Lu and colleagues delivered iTBS600, the intermittent theta-burst protocol used clinically, to mouse organotypic slice cultures and found LTP of excitatory synapses with dendritic spine remodelling persisting for 24 h, while overall network activity stayed stable [114].

<!-- synthesis -->
What "LTP-like" claims in the human work is a resemblance in three properties: the effect follows patterned input, it outlasts the input, and its sign depends on the pattern [38,100]. What it does not claim is a measured synaptic change. The human readout is an MEP, a corticospinal population measure separated from any synapse by a spinal segment and a muscle [38,100]; the slice work that does measure synapses uses a different preparation and, for direct current, supports modulation rather than induction [106,114].

### 6.3 Pharmacological dependencies, and how far they reach

Receptor blockade is the field's main tool for assigning a molecular identity to a human after-effect. Ziemann and colleagues' reference review treats pharmaco-TMS-EMG as a mapping of drug effects onto TMS-evoked muscle responses, and drug challenge as the way to dissect the mechanisms of stimulation-induced LTP-like and LTD-like plasticity [189]. On that framing, a drug-plus-device study is a measurement instrument before it is a treatment [189].

The key result for theta-burst is small. In a placebo-controlled study with six participants, the NMDA antagonist memantine abolished both the MEP suppression after continuous TBS and the facilitation after intermittent TBS, while motor thresholds were unchanged [105]. Genetic evidence points the same way and is less consistent: carriers of the BDNF Met allele responded differently from Val66Val homozygotes across several TMS- and tDCS-based protocols, and replication of such genotype effects has been inconsistent [111]. In slices the molecular dependency is direct: in mouse motor cortex, anodal direct current paired with low-frequency synaptic input produced long-lasting potentiation that required BDNF secretion and TrkB activation, and mice with impaired activity-dependent BDNF release, like humans carrying Val66Met, gained less from motor training [110].

Adding a drug that acts on the gating receptor can multiply a clinical effect. In a randomised placebo-controlled trial in 50 participants with major depressive disorder, four weeks of iTBS with D-cycloserine 100 mg during the first two weeks produced a 6.15-point greater MADRS reduction than placebo (Hedges g = 0.99), with response 73.9% versus 29.3% and remission 39.1% versus 4.2% [190].

<!-- synthesis -->
The blockade logic has a structural limit. That memantine abolishes an after-effect in six people establishes that the after-effect needs NMDA receptors somewhere in the measured pathway [105]; it does not establish where, and the pathway includes spinal and muscular stages as well as cortex [38,105]. The same limit appears in animal work on gating: cholinergic lesion of the nucleus basalis abolished vagus-nerve-stimulation-driven cortical map expansion in rats with equivalent motor performance [141], yet clinically dosed oxybutynin, prazosin and duloxetine did not block that map plasticity [142]. Receptor-level blockade and source-nucleus lesion are not interchangeable evidence for the same edge [141,142]. The corpus for this volume also contains no pooled analysis of the BDNF genotype effect on stimulation response [111].

### 6.4 State dependence: the same dose, different sign

Silvanto and colleagues state the principle: the behavioural and perceptual effects of TMS depend on the activation state of the targeted neurons at the time of stimulation, and setting that state beforehand can bias which of two spatially overlapping populations is affected [104]. The strongest human demonstration that prior activity sets the sign is the preconditioning result. Siebner and colleagues applied tDCS before 1 Hz repetitive TMS over motor cortex: after anodal tDCS had raised excitability, the rTMS pushed excitability below baseline, and after cathodal tDCS had lowered it, the same rTMS raised it [101]. They read the reversals as a homeostatic, BCM-like mechanism stabilising corticospinal excitability [101]. Ziemann and Siebner separated two uses of this: gating, which briefly raises excitability during practice by reducing intracortical inhibition, and homeostatic metaplasticity, which lowers the plasticity threshold by first reducing activity [102]. A consensus paper set the criteria for calling a human result homeostatic at all [103].

State can also be read instant by instant. Zrenner and colleagues used millisecond-latency real-time EEG to trigger otherwise identical triple-pulse bursts at either the negative or the positive peak of the sensorimotor mu-rhythm; bursts locked to the high-excitability negative peak produced LTP-like MEP potentiation, while the same protocol locked to the low-excitability positive peak produced no change [137]. The direction of the outcome was set by the endogenous state rather than by the stimulation parameters [137]. The result is readout-dependent and contested: a companion study found that phase-triggered burst rTMS produced no detectable change in resting EEG or TMS-EEG measures, and concluded that EEG excitability measures do not track corticospinal excitability [213]. Replication across laboratories is thin [137]. Under the third-factor account reviewed by Brzosko and colleagues, this conditionality is expected: acetylcholine, dopamine, noradrenaline and serotonin gate spike-timing-dependent plasticity mechanistically in rodent and in vitro preparations [147].

### 6.5 Variability, and one account reinterpreted as peripheral

Response direction is unreliable between people. López-Alonso and colleagues tested 56 healthy people with paired associative stimulation, anodal tDCS and intermittent TBS: only 39%, 45% and 43% respectively moved in the expected direction, responses were bimodal, and response to one protocol did not reliably predict response to another [204]. The pooled analysis is consistent with this. Across individual data from 430 healthy participants in 22 studies, between-person variability in TBS response was large; iTBS response was predicted by lower baseline MEP amplitude, older age, target muscle and time of day, and cTBS response mainly by baseline MEP and measurement timepoint. The authors attributed much of the variability to measurement method and called for standardisation [107]. The predictors are real; the group effects they explain are small [107].

At the behavioural level the scepticism is sharper. A quantitative review of 59 studies found no significant effect of single-session tDCS on any cognitive outcome in healthy adults, including working memory and language [108]. That review's own inclusion and pooling choices were themselves subsequently contested [108].

The most consequential challenge is a reassignment of level rather than a null. Majdi and colleagues re-examined six published tDCS studies whose effects had been attributed to direct cortical polarization, and argued that stimulation of scalp cranial and cervical nerves could explain them instead (group H-tdcs-plasticity) [109]. The precedent came from alternating current: in rats and humans, anaesthetising the skin reduced tACS-induced tremor entrainment and peripheral nerve stimulation alone reproduced the motor effects [54]. That peripheral account is itself contested — in macaque hippocampus and visual cortex, neurons remained entrained to tACS after topical anaesthesia blocked somatosensory input [55]. What would settle the tDCS case is the design the reinterpretation asks for: transcranial and transcutaneous routes manipulated independently in the same participants [109].

The same frame is applied to drugs and is disputed there too. Kavalali and Monteggia argue that fast-acting antidepressants such as ketamine act through rapid global homeostatic plasticity rather than input-specific Hebbian plasticity [120]; the observation they build on is that in 18 patients with treatment-resistant depression, intravenous ketamine 0.5 mg/kg improved Hamilton scores within 110 minutes with benefit persisting about a week [166]. The locus can also move: in CRISPR knockout mice, psychedelic-induced synaptic strengthening occurred in granular retrosplenial cortex, whose neurons lack postsynaptic 5-HT2A receptors, and required presynaptic 5-HT2A receptors on anterior thalamic axons instead [174].

### 6.6 The cells that are not neurons

Astrocytes respond to transcranial direct current on a larger scale than local neurons do. In transgenic mice, tDCS produced widespread astrocytic Ca²⁺ surges across cortex with little change in local neuronal activity, and the subsequent enhancement of sensory-evoked cortical responses required α1-adrenergic signalling and IP3R2-dependent astrocytic Ca²⁺ [121]. In mouse, that is an alternative to a purely neuronal polarization account [121].

For microglia the evidence is a dispute. In Alzheimer's model mice, optogenetic 40 Hz driving of fast-spiking interneurons lowered Aβ1-40 and Aβ1-42 and changed microglial morphology, and non-invasive 40 Hz flicker reduced amyloid in visual cortex [123]; a later study in the same model reported that 40 Hz audiovisual stimulation increased CSF influx and interstitial efflux with changes in astrocytic AQP4 polarization, and that blocking glymphatic clearance abolished amyloid removal [124]. Against both, large-scale recordings in visual cortex and hippocampus of model mice found that 40 Hz flicker did not entrain native gamma oscillations, that the mice avoided the flicker, and that plaques and microglia were not meaningfully changed [125]. In primates the stimulation result is null: macaques given cTBS or iTBS over motor cortex five days a week for three to four weeks showed no significant change in [¹¹C]PBR28 TSPO PET or MR spectroscopy markers of glial activation or neuronal injury [126].

<!-- synthesis -->
These results place glia in the chain as participants for at least one drive, in rodents [121,124], while the only primate-level assay of stimulation-driven glial activation collected here is negative [126]. The cellular level's responding unit is therefore not established to be the neuron alone, nor established to be anything else [121,125,126].

### 6.7 What this level requires of a graph

<!-- synthesis -->
"Plasticity" cannot be one edge. The same word covers an in vivo rabbit field potential [96], a multiplicative change in quantal amplitude in rat culture [99], a spine count in mouse organotypic slice [114] and an MEP change in a human forearm muscle [38,100]. An edge is only interpretable if it carries the induction protocol with its parameters, the readout, the preparation and species, and the direction [38,96,99,114].

<!-- synthesis -->
Direction needs a distribution, not a sign. When 39–45% of participants move the expected way and responses are bimodal [204], and when the same protocol's outcome flips with prior stimulation [101] or with the phase at which it is delivered [137], a single signed edge asserts something the data do not support. The graph should store the responder fraction and the measured predictors — baseline MEP amplitude, age, target muscle, time of day [107] — as properties of the edge, and represent the contested cases (H-tdcs-plasticity) as rival edges at different levels, cortical [106,110] and peripheral [54,109], rather than resolving them.

## 7. Where the modalities converge

<!-- framing -->
This section takes the subcellular and molecular level: the short list of endpoints at which drives with nothing physical in common are said to meet, each taken in turn, and then one convergence claim followed all the way through its dispute.

### 7.1 The claim, and the two ways it goes wrong

Above this level the modalities share nothing: a direct current shapes a membrane potential, a photon is absorbed by an enzyme, a ligand occupies a binding site. At this level the same names keep appearing. Anodal direct current paired with low-frequency synaptic input potentiates mouse motor cortex slices only if BDNF is secreted and TrkB is activated [110]. Ketamine at a single dose raises mTOR signalling in rat prefrontal cortex and adds dendritic spines [115]. Near-infrared light restores the activity of cytochrome c oxidase in primary neuron cultures poisoned with cyanide [88]. Repetitive TMS changes c-Fos and zif268 protein levels in rat cortex [112]. Five names — BDNF–TrkB, mTOR, the immediate-early genes, mitochondrial state, and glial and inflammatory signalling — carry most of the traffic.

<!-- synthesis -->
That recurrence is the reason to build one graph rather than one per modality: if the endpoints are shared, a finding about a drug constrains what to expect of a device, and the chain from drive to outcome can be joined across modality families at this level [110,115,122]. It is also where overstatement is cheapest, in two specific ways.

<!-- figure: fig5 -->

<!-- synthesis -->
The first is that a shared marker is not a shared mechanism. BDNF protein can rise because activity-dependent secretion was potentiated, as in the direct-current slice work [110], or because NMDA receptor blockade at rest deactivated eEF2 kinase and released BDNF messenger RNA for translation, as reported in mice given ketamine [116]. Those are different edges with the same head node, and an increase in BDNF distinguishes neither [110,116]. The second is that a marker measured in different preparations is not the same measurement. Translocator protein (TSPO) rises in activated microglia in mouse models but not in non-human primates or in human neurodegenerative disease, a gap traced to an AP1 site in the promoter, so a human TSPO-PET signal is better read as the density of inflammatory cells than as their activation state [127]. A convergence edge assembled from a mouse immunostain and a human PET scan is therefore assembled from two quantities that are not the same quantity [127].

### 7.2 BDNF and TrkB

BDNF is the field's most cited convergence point, and the evidence for it is of several different kinds. In mouse motor cortex slices, anodal direct current stimulation paired with low-frequency synaptic input produced long-lasting synaptic potentiation that required BDNF secretion and TrkB activation; mice with impaired activity-dependent BDNF release, and humans carrying the BDNF Val66Met variant, gained less from motor skill training [110]. In humans the evidence is genotype-level and indirect: Met allele carriers responded differently from Val66Val homozygotes across several TMS- and tDCS-based plasticity protocols, and replication of such genotype effects has been inconsistent [111].

The strongest version of the convergence claim is stronger than "both routes raise BDNF". Casarotto and colleagues report that the TRKB transmembrane domain senses cholesterol and that both classical and rapid-acting antidepressants bind a site in the transmembrane region of TRKB dimers, promoting synaptic localization of the receptor and BDNF signalling; mutating the binding site reduced cellular and mouse behavioural antidepressant responses [118]. The same group extended the proposal to psychedelics, reporting that LSD and psilocin bind TrkB with high affinity at sites partly distinct from the antidepressant site, and that in mice the plasticity-promoting and antidepressant-like effects required TrkB but not 5-HT2A receptors, while the head-twitch response required 5-HT2A [119]. On that account TrkB is not a downstream marker but a direct drug target shared across chemical classes [118,119].

Both papers combine molecular modelling, cell work and mouse behaviour, and both come from one laboratory [118,119]. The psychedelic version competes with the account in which 5-HT2A does the work: intracellular rather than cell-surface 5-HT2A receptors mediate cortical plasticity, which is why membrane-impermeant serotonin does not engage the mechanism despite being a 5-HT2A agonist [92]. A current review separates the two questions usually conflated — whether 5-HT2A activation is necessary for the hallucinogenic effect, and whether it is necessary for the plasticity effect — and treats the second as open [173]. The locus can also sit in the afferent cell: in CRISPR knockout mice, psychedelic-induced synaptic strengthening occurred in granular retrosplenial cortex, whose neurons lack postsynaptic 5-HT2A receptors, and required presynaptic 5-HT2A receptors on anterior thalamic axons [174].

<!-- synthesis -->
The corpus for this volume contains no independent replication of the TRKB transmembrane binding site by a laboratory other than the one that proposed it [118,119]. Until there is one, "antidepressants and psychedelics converge on TrkB" should be held as a single-laboratory structural claim with mouse behavioural support, not as the established mechanism it is often cited as [118,119,173].

### 7.3 mTOR and rapid synaptogenesis

The mTOR branch comes from the rapid-acting antidepressant work and carries an explicit timescale. Li and colleagues found that in rats a single ketamine dose quickly activated mTOR signalling in prefrontal cortex, raised synaptic signalling proteins, and increased the number and function of dendritic spines; rapamycin blocked both the synaptogenesis and the antidepressant-like behaviour [115]. The human anchor for the timescale is separate and behavioural: in seven patients with major depression given intravenous ketamine 0.5 mg/kg in a placebo-controlled crossover, 25-item Hamilton scores fell by about 14 points within 72 hours [165].

Within the pharmacology, which molecular step is required is contested. The metabolite account holds that (2R,6R)-hydroxynorketamine produces antidepressant-like effects in mice without NMDA receptor inhibition and with a requirement for AMPA receptor activation [117]; a systematic target deconvolution across binding, functional and large-scale screens at thousands of human proteins found no direct target for that metabolite at all [167]. Against both, the same laboratory that proposed the metabolite later argued for convergence downstream: in male mice, ketamine showed an inverted-U dose-response (effective at 10 mg/kg, ineffective higher), and pretreatment with MK-801 or CPP abolished the behavioural effect of ketamine, of (2R,6R)-hydroxynorketamine, of a GABA-A modulator and of an mGlu2/3 antagonist alike, which the authors read as a shared requirement for downstream NMDA receptor activation [168]. A competing framing is that the relevant plasticity is homeostatic rather than Hebbian, with fast-acting antidepressants triggering rapid homeostatic synaptic plasticity [120].

<!-- synthesis -->
The mTOR and eEF2K findings are rat and mouse, at doses given in mg/kg by injection, with spine counts and phosphorylation states as readouts [115,116]. The corpus for this volume contains no measurement of mTOR activation or spine addition in a human brain after any drive, device or drug [115,116,165]. The human end of this branch is a symptom score, and the join to the molecular end is inference.

### 7.4 Immediate-early genes and activity markers

Immediate-early gene expression is the workhorse readout of the level, because it is cheap, anatomically resolved and sensitive to pattern. In rats, 1 Hz and 10 Hz repetitive TMS raised c-Fos in all cortical areas tested, whereas intermittent theta-burst stimulation raised it only in limbic cortices, so the molecular response tracked the stimulation pattern rather than the total number of pulses [112]. The modern version has more resolution and the same logic: spatial transcriptomics in mice after two low-intensity rTMS protocols showed changes in plasticity-related and cellular-process genes across many regions, and in cortex those changes depended on region, layer and protocol [113].

What these readouts cannot do is give the direction of a synaptic change. c-Fos and zif268 protein levels mark that tissue was driven, and the two protocols that raised c-Fos everywhere in that rat experiment, 1 Hz and 10 Hz, were not distinguished from each other by the marker [112]. Where direction is needed the measurement has to be synaptic: in mouse organotypic slice cultures the clinical iTBS600 protocol produced LTP of excitatory synapses with spine remodelling lasting 24 hours while network activity stayed stable [114].

### 7.5 Mitochondrial and metabolic endpoints

For one modality the mitochondrion is not a downstream endpoint but the proposed transducer. Competing 670 nm LED light against potassium cyanide in primary neuron cultures, light partially restored cytochrome c oxidase activity blocked by 10–100 µM cyanide and cut cell death from 83.6% to 43.5% at 300 µM, with no protection at millimolar concentrations, and efficacy across 670, 728, 770, 830 and 880 nm tracked the absorption spectrum of the oxidised enzyme [88]. The signalling step proposed to follow is photodissociation of nitric oxide: in HeLa cells irradiated at 600–860 nm, adding nitric oxide donors before exposure shifted the action spectrum for cell attachment in a donor- and fluence-dependent way [89].

Other modalities arrive at metabolism from outside. Rats given bilateral anodal tDCS to parietal cortex at 200 µA for 20 min per day for five days showed metabolomic and transcriptomic changes in glycolysis, mitochondrial function and calcium-related signalling in the stimulated tissue [122]. The rodent current density is far above the values used in humans, so the dose does not transfer directly [122].

### 7.6 Glia and neuroinflammation, as endpoint and as effector

Glia appear on both sides of the arrow. As effector, astrocytes are reported to be the acoustic transducer: low-intensity low-frequency ultrasound opens astrocytic TRPA1, and the resulting calcium entry drives glutamate release through Best1 channels onto neuronal NMDA receptors, in rodent and in vitro preparations [69]. For direct current in mice, tDCS produced widespread cortical astrocytic Ca²⁺ surges with little change in local neuronal firing, and the subsequent sensory-response enhancement required α1-adrenergic signalling and IP3R2-dependent astrocytic Ca²⁺ [121].

As endpoint, inflammation is where a coupling choice shows its cost, and the reading is disputed. Pulsed focused ultrasound with systemic microbubbles produced damage-associated molecular patterns and a parenchymal sterile inflammatory response lasting about 24 hours in rodents, with a profile the authors compared to ischaemia or mild traumatic brain injury [83]. Against the generality of that conclusion, a rat study comparing three sonication protocols at different microbubble doses found no significant NF-κB pathway change at clinical imaging doses, while high doses activated the pathway and produced oedema, neuronal degeneration, neutrophil infiltration and microhaemorrhage at 6 hours and 4 days [84]. The dispute is therefore about dose, not about whether inflammation can occur [83,84]. Outside the brain the same endpoint is the outcome: vagus nerve stimulation in rats during lethal endotoxaemia inhibited hepatic TNF synthesis and prevented shock [148], with splenic choline acetyltransferase-expressing T cells required as a relay [150].

### 7.7 A worked example: 40 Hz sensory stimulation

The 40 Hz case is the clearest instance of a convergence claim whose links are separately contested. The original chain ran from a sensory or optogenetic drive through gamma entrainment to microglia and amyloid: in Alzheimer's model mice, optogenetic 40 Hz driving of fast-spiking interneurons lowered Aβ1-40 and Aβ1-42 and changed microglial morphology, and non-invasive 40 Hz light flicker reduced amyloid in visual cortex [123]. A later study in a mouse model added a different effector for the same drive, reporting that 40 Hz audiovisual stimulation increased CSF influx and interstitial fluid efflux with changes in astrocytic AQP4 polarization and meningeal lymphatic vessels, and that blocking glymphatic clearance abolished amyloid removal [124]. Against both, large-scale recordings in visual cortex and hippocampus of model mice found that 40 Hz flicker did not entrain native gamma oscillations, that the mice avoided the flicker, and that plaques and microglia were not meaningfully changed [125].

<!-- synthesis -->
Two things follow. The chain has at least four joins — drive to entrainment, entrainment to glial state, glial state to clearance, clearance to plaque load — and the supporting papers do not all measure the same ones: the glymphatic study supplies a mechanism for clearance while the counter-study attacks the first join [123–125]. And the assay problem set out above applies directly, since the only primate-level test of stimulation-driven glial activation collected here is a null obtained with a marker that does not index activation in primates: macaques given cTBS or iTBS over motor cortex five days a week for three to four weeks showed no significant change in [¹¹C]PBR28 TSPO PET or MR spectroscopy [126,127]. What would settle the first join is a replication that reports entrainment measured invasively and amyloid in the same animals, with flicker aversion controlled [125].

### 7.8 What this level requires of a graph

<!-- synthesis -->
An endpoint node cannot be the molecule. "BDNF" as a node collapses secreted protein in a mouse slice [110], translated protein after eEF2K deactivation in mouse cortex [116], a human genotype [111], and a structural binding claim about its receptor [118]. The node has to carry the assay, the preparation and species, and the direction of change, or the edges into it are not comparable [110,111,116,118].

<!-- synthesis -->
Convergence edges are weaker than they look unless the assay is shared. Two drives that both "raise BDNF", measured by different assays in different species, support a much weaker join than two drives whose effects are abolished by the same intervention in the same preparation — which is the form of the strongest convergence evidence collected here, the shared NMDA-receptor-activation requirement across four unrelated compounds in mice [168], the cholinergic lesion that abolishes device-driven map plasticity in rats [141], and the D-cycloserine addition that multiplied an iTBS clinical effect in humans [190]. The graph should mark which kind of evidence each convergence edge rests on, and should record when it rests on one laboratory [118,119], and it should hold the gating relation separately, since plasticity is conditional on a modulatory third factor rather than following from the drive alone [147].

## 8. Circuits, networks and the neuromodulator systems

<!-- framing -->
Two questions are run together throughout this literature and kept apart here: where to deliver a drive, and what the drive does once it has arrived. The section takes the circuit-level vocabulary, then the mechanism dispute for high-frequency deep brain stimulation, then biomarkers and closed-loop control, then connectivity-based targeting, then the ascending neuromodulator systems and the proxies used to claim they were engaged.

### 8.1 From neuron to circuit: oscillation, phase, coherence

The circuit level has three working terms. Fries' account supplies the reason they are treated as control variables rather than by-products: active neuronal groups oscillate, so their excitability fluctuates rhythmically, opening and closing short windows for receiving input and emitting output, and two groups influence one another effectively only when their windows align at a consistent phase relation [128]. A changing pattern of coherence is then a changing effective connectivity, reconfigurable on cognitive timescales [128]. This is theory, and it licenses entrainment-style intervention: if phase gates communication, imposing phase should change routing [128].

Whether an applied field can impose that phase is a separate, measured question. In ferret cortex, endogenous fields during physiological activity reached about 2–4 mV/mm, and in ferret cortical slices applied fields of that in-vivo-like amplitude sped up, regularised and entrained the slow oscillation [37]. Network modelling attributed the size of the effect to small, simultaneous depolarisation of many neurons rather than strong drive of any one — a computational attribution, where the field amplitudes are slice measurements [37].

Awake primate recordings take the claim closer to the human case. Scalp transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS) in macaques altered the timing but not the rate of single-neuron spiking, including in hippocampus and basal ganglia [51]; a published comment attributes that result to a peripheral mechanism, which the authors answered [51]. Entrainment is conditional on what the neuron was already doing: tACS entrained neurons weakly locked to an ongoing oscillation, and often reduced phase locking in neurons already strongly locked to an endogenous rhythm, which the authors propose as a source of inconsistent human tACS outcomes [44]. Simulations with supporting ferret recordings add the resonance condition: frequency-specific enhancement was largest when stimulation frequency matched the network's intrinsic rhythm [53].

<!-- synthesis -->
The circuit-level vocabulary therefore arrives with an interaction term: efficacy is a function of the applied drive and the ongoing state jointly, not of the drive alone [37,44,53]. The supporting results are ferret slice, simulation and macaque single-unit, and the coherence framework is theory [37,44,51,53,128]; the corpus for this volume contains no human study measuring imposed phase at the single-neuron level.

### 8.2 What high-frequency deep brain stimulation does to a circuit

Grill and colleagues modelled intrinsically active neurons under extracellular stimulation across frequencies and found that high-frequency stimulation forces output spiking into a regular, essentially variance-free pattern: the cell keeps firing but stops transmitting information [133]. This informational lesion reconciles two facts that neither simple inhibition nor simple excitation explains — that clinical benefit requires high frequency, and that the clinical picture resembles a lesion even though axonal output is being driven [133]. The account is computational, and puts the therapeutic effect in removal of pathological patterning, not a change of mean rate [133].

The rival pole holds the opposite of silencing: high-frequency stimulation excites efferent axons and recruits cortex antidromically, with network-level desynchronisation as a related variant, and the dispute is unadjudicated [133]. Human intraoperative and chronic recordings show the stimulated nucleus is not quiet: DBS pulses evoke a large, focal resonant response within the subthalamic nucleus, largest in the dorsal STN that is the clinically preferred target, present under general anaesthesia, and associated with better clinical performance [134].

<!-- synthesis -->
Outcome data do not separate the two accounts, because both predict benefit at high frequency [133]. They differ in what they predict about the nucleus's output: variance-free driven firing carrying no information, versus recruitment of projection axons carrying a new signal to cortex [133,134]. Evoked resonant activity is consistent with driving, but it is a stimulation-locked population response and does not measure information content [134]. Settling this needs efferent firing pattern and downstream decoding measured together, under parameter sets that are and are not clinically effective [133,134].

### 8.3 Biomarkers and closed-loop control

If timing relative to state is part of the effect, a state signal can control delivery. In eight patients with advanced Parkinson's disease, subthalamic beta-band local field potentials from the stimulating electrode gated stimulation online, so current flowed only when a pathological beta burst crossed threshold [135]. Blinded motor scores improved 50% with adaptive stimulation, about 27% better than conventional continuous stimulation, while total stimulation time fell by 56%; random intermittent stimulation did not match it, which locates the benefit in the timing rather than in intermittency itself [135].

The control signal is neither fixed across patients nor necessarily endogenous. A blinded randomised cross-over feasibility trial in four patients identified stimulation-entrained gamma oscillations in STN or motor cortex as the best individual markers of high- versus low-dopaminergic states, then used those signals to drive chronic adaptive DBS at home, improving motor symptoms and quality of life relative to clinically optimised continuous stimulation [136]. Stimulation-evoked responses serve target verification rather than control, a different use of the same signal class [134].

Non-invasively the equivalent is phase-triggered stimulation, and its record includes a null. With millisecond-latency real-time EEG triggering identical repetitive triple-pulse TMS bursts, bursts locked to the high-excitability negative peak of the sensorimotor mu-rhythm produced LTP-like potentiation of motor evoked potentials, while the same protocol locked to the low-excitability positive peak produced no change [137]. A companion study triggering burst rTMS at a mu-rhythm phase found no detectable change in resting EEG or TMS-EEG measures of excitability, and concluded that EEG excitability measures do not track corticospinal excitability [213]. The effect is readout-dependent, and cross-laboratory replication remains thin [137,213].

### 8.4 Connectivity-based targeting: where to stimulate

The paradigm begins with a retrospective correlation. Using resting-state fMRI from 98 healthy subjects, Fox and colleagues computed the connectivity fingerprint of the left dorsolateral prefrontal coil positions used in published rTMS depression trials; sites with better reported efficacy were more strongly anticorrelated with the subgenual cingulate [129]. They inverted this into a targeting rule, deriving optimal coordinates in BA46, and showed the same pattern in an independent sample of 13 depressed patients [129]. Efficacy was taken from the literature rather than measured in these subjects, so the design is correlational and retrospective [129].

The connectomic extension moved the unit of analysis from the coil to the stimulated volume. In 51 patients with subthalamic DBS for Parkinson's disease, electrode localisation combined with normative human connectome data showed structural and functional connectivity of the stimulation volume to be independent predictors of motor improvement, with supplementary-motor-area connectivity and anticorrelation with M1 prominent; the same profile predicted outcome in an independent cohort, without patient-specific imaging [132]. Pooling 14 datasets of lesions (n=461), TMS sites (n=151) and DBS sites (n=101), circuits derived from damage and from therapeutic stimulation converged on one distributed network independent of diagnosis, and connectivity to it predicted antidepressant efficacy of held-out TMS and DBS sites [131].

The replication challenge is specific about size. In 295 patients treated with rTMS for depression, connectivity between the actual stimulation site and the subgenual ACC was associated with outcome only weakly, at r about −0.16, and only when targets were defined by electric-field modelling rather than nominal coil position; much of the association tracked respiration-driven artefact in the global signal [130]. The authors put sgACC connectivity at roughly 3% of outcome variance, too little to support current individualised targeting protocols on its own [130].

<!-- synthesis -->
The dispute is not whether connectivity carries information about outcome but how much, and under which definition of the target [129,130]. The same measure yields a usable targeting rule when the target is a literature-derived coordinate [129] and about 3% of variance when it is the tissue an E-field model says was stimulated [130]. Target definition is part of the claim, not a detail behind it [129,130,132].

### 8.5 The ascending neuromodulator systems: target and gate

Aston-Jones and Cohen's adaptive-gain account, synthesising primate electrophysiology with computational modelling, holds that the locus coeruleus sets neural gain rather than merely arousal, in two regimes: phasic responses time-locked to task-relevant decisions support task engagement, while elevated tonic firing accompanies disengagement and favours exploration [138]. Modern circuit work qualifies the broadcast picture: LC subpopulations encode specific processes and have target-specific innervation and heterogeneous receptor distributions, so LC activation has more selective network consequences than a diffuse model implies [139].

Device engagement of these systems has been made parametric. Rat LC recordings across a grid of amplitude, frequency, train duration, inter-train interval and pulse width showed half-second vagus-nerve stimulation trains driving phasic LC firing from 0.1 mA; higher current and longer pulse widths raised LC firing rate, pulse frequency changed the timing of the burst without changing total LC activity, and pulse-locked trigeminal activity appeared only above 1.2 mA [140]. In humans, release has been measured with a competition assay: left dorsolateral prefrontal rTMS reduced 11C-raclopride binding in the ipsilateral dorsal caudate only, with no change in putamen, accumbens or contralateral caudate, implying focal dopamine release remote from the stimulated cortex [143].

The gate claim is that plasticity is conditional on these systems. Acetylcholine, dopamine, noradrenaline and serotonin gate spike-timing-dependent plasticity mechanistically in rodent and in vitro preparations [147]. The causal device-side result is a lesion study: pairing forelimb training with VNS expanded the proximal forelimb motor representation in rats with intact nucleus basalis but not after selective lesion of its cholinergic neurons, with motor performance equivalent across groups [141]. The necessity claim is challenged at the receptor level: rats dosed daily with oxybutynin, prazosin or duloxetine at clinically relevant doses, while VNS was paired with a jaw movement, all showed the expected VNS-dependent map expansion [142]. Pharmacological blockade and lesion of the source nucleus are not interchangeable evidence for the same edge [141,142].

Drugs act on this level too, and inherit the same problem of locating the action. Pulsatile versus continuous levodopa exposure of one receptor population gives different long-term outcomes [186]. For psychedelics the site is disputed: in CRISPR knockout mice, synaptic strengthening in granular retrosplenial cortex, whose neurons lack postsynaptic 5-HT2A receptors, required presynaptic 5-HT2A receptors on anterior thalamic axons [174], against the standard account placing the effect at 5-HT2A receptors on layer V pyramidal apical dendrites [172].

### 8.6 Proxies, and whether they index what they are used for

Pupil diameter is the standard non-invasive stand-in for noradrenergic engagement. Simultaneous LC spike recording and pupillometry in mice found a monotonic, graded relationship between LC spike count and dilation, but with enough variability that pupil size predicted only a small fraction of moment-to-moment LC activity; responses to identical optogenetic LC stimulation varied strongly between sessions, and that variation tracked decision-bias-related behaviour, so the pupil–LC mapping is itself state-dependent [144].

The human record matches that weakness, then partitions it by protocol. In 66 healthy participants, continuous cymba conchae transcutaneous auricular VNS at an intensity titrated just below pain threshold left event-related pupil dilation, resting pupil size, cortisol and salivary alpha-amylase unchanged relative to earlobe sham, while the task itself produced the expected pupil effects [145]. A Bayesian meta-analysis of 18 taVNS studies (N=771) found only anecdotal evidence overall (g=0.15, BF01=1.0), splitting sharply by protocol: pulsed stimulation gave strong evidence for an effect (g=0.36, BF10=50.8) and continuous stimulation strong evidence for the null (g=0.002, BF01=21.9) [146]. That split maps onto the phasic–tonic distinction in the adaptive-gain account and onto the parameter dependence measured in rat LC [138,140,146].

### 8.7 What this level implies for a graph

<!-- framing -->
The section's two questions come back apart here, as three things an edge has to carry.

<!-- synthesis -->
A circuit-level edge cannot be written as drive → effect. It needs the target definition attached, because the same connectivity measure supports a targeting rule under one definition and explains 3% of variance under another [129,130]; anatomical, functional and connectivity-derived targets are different nodes, not labels for one node [131,132]. It needs the state the brain was in, because entrainment reverses sign with prior phase locking [44] and the direction of a plasticity outcome is set by the phase at delivery rather than by the parameters [137]. And it needs the biomarker named, because the same protocol potentiates a corticospinal readout while leaving an EEG readout unchanged [137,213], and because a closed loop's control signal may be endogenous beta [135] or stimulation-entrained gamma that exists only while stimulating [136]. Where a proxy stands in for a system, the edge inherits the proxy's validity, which for pupil and noradrenaline is weak and protocol-dependent [144,146].

<!-- synthesis -->
One requirement follows from the gate claims. A statement that a device effect requires a neuromodulator system is only as strong as the manipulation behind it: source-nucleus lesion and receptor antagonism give opposite answers for the same VNS plasticity effect [141,142], and for a drug the responding cell may not express the receptor [174]. The necessity edge carries the perturbation method, not just a sign.

## 9. Beyond the brain: autonomic, immune, endocrine, interoceptive

<!-- framing -->
This section follows one chain from an applied drive to a molecule and an outcome, then the argument about whether that chain is a reflex.

### Why the chain closes here and not in the head

The neuroimmune branch has something the cortical branches do not: the drive is a nerve that can be reached surgically and stimulated with known current, the readout is a molecule assayable in serum, and the anatomy between them is a named set of organs and projections. Borovikova and colleagues' experiment ran from an electrical drive in the rat to hepatic and serum TNF and to survival in endotoxaemia [148]. That is the whole chain of section 2 — drive, coupling, cellular, molecular, system, outcome — in one preparation.

Tracey's formalisation treats inflammatory state as something the nervous system senses and regulates in real time, as it regulates heart rate, which makes cytokine concentration a controlled variable and therefore a target for a device [149]. Pavlov and Tracey's later review sets out which neural, endocrine and immune nodes connect to which, and which of those connections have been addressed experimentally [154].

### The inflammatory reflex as it was demonstrated

Borovikova and colleagues reported two experiments in one paper [148]. In human macrophages stimulated with lipopolysaccharide *in vitro*, acetylcholine strongly suppressed release of TNF, IL-1β, IL-6 and IL-18, but not IL-10 [148]. In rats made endotoxaemic, direct electrical stimulation of the peripheral vagus nerve inhibited hepatic TNF synthesis, blunted the peak of serum TNF and prevented shock [148]. The authors read this as an efferent, parasympathetic anti-inflammatory pathway, distinct from the already-described afferent vagal route that acts through the HPA axis [148].

<!-- synthesis -->
Dose is a parameter set and not a dial (section 3), and the record this volume works from does not carry the current, frequency, pulse width or train duration of that rat stimulation [148]. The founding experiment therefore cannot be replicated from what is cited here, and the parametric work that makes a vagal drive reproducible was done later and for a different readout [140].

That parametric work is instructive about how narrow the window is. Recording in rat locus coeruleus across a grid of current amplitude, pulse frequency, train duration, inter-train interval and pulse width, Hulsey and colleagues found that half-second vagus-nerve stimulation trains drove rapid phasic locus coeruleus firing from 0.1 mA upward; higher current and longer pulse widths raised the firing rate, while pulse frequency changed the timing of the phasic burst without changing the total amount of locus coeruleus activity [140]. Pulse-locked activity in the mesencephalic trigeminal nucleus, included as a control for non-specific recruitment, appeared only above 1.2 mA [140]. The same cuff at two amplitudes is therefore two different drives, one of which co-recruits trigeminal afferents [140].

### The relay that filled in the middle

The efferent account had an anatomical hole: vagal suppression of splenic TNF requires acetylcholine acting on α7 nicotinic receptors on the cytokine-producing macrophages, but vagal fibres do not innervate the spleen and lack the machinery to synthesise acetylcholine there [150]. Rosas-Ballina and colleagues closed the gap in mice with a non-neural cell. They identified a memory-phenotype population of choline acetyltransferase-expressing T cells in the spleen, and showed that this population is required for vagus-nerve stimulation to inhibit TNF [150]. The efferent arc thus passes through a lymphocyte that makes the transmitter, and terminates on a receptor expressed by a macrophage [150].

<!-- synthesis -->
For the graph, this is the section's most consequential single result: an edge in a drive-to-outcome chain can have a non-neuronal cell at one end, so the schema cannot restrict nodes to neurons, axons and brain regions [150]. Gut interoception supplies a second such node type [158].

### What the anatomy does and does not support

The reflex framing is where the agreement stops. Martelli, McKinley and McAllen audited the evidence point by point and accepted a great deal of it: that stimulating the cut peripheral end of either vagus suppresses local and systemic inflammation, that the spleen is where systemic TNF is produced and suppressed, that the effect requires noradrenergic nerve terminals in the spleen and ChAT-positive T lymphocytes, and that α7 nicotinic receptors are essential [151]. What they rejected is the proposed disynaptic connection from vagus to splenic nerve, on the ground that vagal stimulation does not drive action potentials in the splenic nerve [151]. Their conclusion separates two claims that the reflex language fuses: the pathway is activatable, electrically and pharmacologically, but it is not the efferent arm of a physiological reflex to endotoxaemia [151].

<!-- synthesis -->
The dispute is therefore not about whether a device works but about what the device is doing to what. One side holds that stimulation engages a hard-wired homeostatic loop that already regulates cytokines [149,150]; the other holds that stimulation drives a pathway that exists but is not recruited by endotoxaemia itself, in which case the intervention is closer to a pharmacological trick played on splenic innervation than to closing a natural loop [151]. What would settle it is electrophysiology rather than outcome data: recordings showing that splenic nerve traffic is driven by vagal stimulation, and that it changes when an animal is made inflamed with the vagus intact. The first has been attempted and reported negative [151].

### The human evidence, and what it does and does not establish

The human literature is thinner than the animal literature and differently shaped. Alen's review of vagally mediated control of inflammation in humans finds a large correlational body of work, mostly associations between heart-rate-variability indices and cytokine concentrations, against a much smaller set of experimental studies, with weak causal designs, inconsistent measures of vagal activity and limited target-engagement evidence [152].

The pivotal test is an implant trial. RESET-RA randomised 242 patients with rheumatoid arthritis refractory to or intolerant of biologic or targeted synthetic DMARDs to active or sham stimulation from an implanted vagus-nerve neuroimmune modulation device for three months, then moved all participants to open-label stimulation to twelve months [153]. ACR20 response at three months was 35.2% with active stimulation against 24.2% with sham (p = 0.02), and among completers rose to 50.0% at six months and 52.8% at twelve [153]. Adverse events were similar between arms, and the few related serious events were perioperative and resolved [153].

<!-- synthesis -->
An eleven-point difference in responder rate, sham-controlled, in a refractory population, is a real result and a modest one, and it does not adjudicate the mechanism dispute: a benefit of this size is compatible with both the reflex account and the activatable-but-not-reflex account [151,153].

Non-invasive delivery adds a target-engagement problem on top of the mechanism problem. D'Agostini and colleagues gave 66 healthy participants continuous cymba conchae transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation against earlobe sham, at an individually titrated intensity just below pain threshold, and found no effect on event-related pupil dilation, baseline or resting pupil size, cortisol, salivary alpha-amylase, cardiac vagal activity or respiration, while the task itself produced the expected pupil effects [145]. The resolution is protocol-dependent. A living Bayesian meta-analysis of 18 taVNS studies (N = 771) found only anecdotal evidence for a pupil effect overall (g = 0.15, BF01 = 1.0), but strong evidence for an effect with pulsed stimulation (g = 0.36, BF10 = 50.8) and strong evidence for the null with conventional continuous stimulation (g = 0.002, BF01 = 21.9) [146]. That split maps onto the phasic and tonic locus coeruleus regimes of the adaptive-gain account [138], and it makes waveform, not intensity, the decisive parameter [146]. The proxy is itself weak: simultaneous locus coeruleus recording and pupillometry in mice gave a monotonic but highly variable relationship, with pupil size predicting only a small fraction of moment-to-moment locus coeruleus activity [144].

### The sensory limb

Reflex or not, the afferent side is where most vagal fibres are. Prescott and Liberles' review organises vagal sensory neurons by molecularly defined cell type across the respiratory, cardiovascular and digestive systems, and stresses how little is established about signal transduction in vagal afferents and their upstream sentinel cells compared with the external senses [157]. The practical consequence is that "the vagus" is a heterogeneous bundle of genetically distinct afferent classes, so a cuff or an ear electrode engages a mixture of labelled lines rather than a channel [157].

Gut-brain signalling had been assumed to be hormonal and therefore slow, because no synapse was known between the intestinal epithelium and the vagus [158]. Working in mice, Kaelberer and colleagues showed that enteroendocrine cells — neuropod cells, in their term — form glutamatergic synapses with vagal afferents and transduce luminal stimuli in milliseconds, linking the lumen to the brainstem across a single synapse [158].

The endocrine limb closes the same way from the other direction. A meta-analysis of 16 randomised controlled trials of prefrontal TMS or tDCS in healthy adults found that stimulation reduced the acute cortisol response to a laboratory stressor (SMD = −0.72), with larger effects for TMS than tDCS and for stimulation delivered before or after rather than during the stressor [159]. Timing relative to the challenge is part of the dose [159]. In the other direction, certain pregnane steroids act both as circulating endocrine messengers and as locally synthesised neurosteroids enhancing GABA-A receptor function by a direct, non-genomic action, so one molecule can be a systemic drive or a local one [179].

### Anatomy a graph can stand on

SCKAN, the SPARC Connectivity Knowledge Base of the Autonomic Nervous System, is the closest thing this level has to a substrate. It is a curated, ontology-backed record of the origins, terminations and routing of autonomic projections between the central nervous system and end organs, assembled from expert input, published literature, textbooks and SPARC datasets, and it generates the connectivity maps on the SPARC portal [155]. Its neuron populations are named compositionally rather than by opaque label, using the Neuron Phenotype Ontology, which decomposes a neuron type into phenotypes carrying identifiers from community ontologies and reconciled three published cortical neuron classifications with each other and with informal literature names [156]. Organ and structure identifiers come from a species-neutral anatomy ontology that links species-specific ones, so a structure can be compared across humans and model organisms [214].

What SCKAN contains is population-level structural connectivity: which neuron population projects from where to where, by what route [155]. It is not a source of stimulation-response or physiological measurement data. Nothing in it says what a 0.1 mA half-second train does to locus coeruleus firing [140], whether vagal stimulation drives splenic nerve spikes [151], or how large an ACR20 difference an implant produces [153]. Those are separate claims, from separate papers, with their own species and parameters.

### What this level implies for the graph

<!-- synthesis -->
Three schema consequences follow. First, direction belongs on the edge and not in the node name. Afferent and efferent vagal routes have different anatomy, different transducers and different consequences, and the founding paper distinguishes its efferent pathway from an afferent HPA route in the same nerve [148,157]. An edge that says only "vagus → spleen" has lost the claim. Second, sign of effect belongs on the branch rather than on the drive. Vagal stimulation suppresses TNF through one branch [148,150] and raises locus coeruleus firing through another [140]; "vagus nerve stimulation" carries no sign of its own. Third, species must survive import. The molecular chain is rodent with human *in vitro* pharmacology [148,150], the critique is rodent electrophysiology [151], the clinical result is human [153], the human mechanism literature is largely correlational [152], and the anatomy resource is multi-species by construction [155,214]. A graph that imports SCKAN's structural statements alongside stimulation-response claims must keep the evidence type on every edge, which is what an evidence ontology is for [218], or it will read a curated anatomical projection and a sham-controlled trial as the same kind of fact.

## 10. The map of techniques

<!-- framing -->
The sections before this one took the coupling routes one at a time. This one sets the human-applicable families side by side in one table. The prose says what the comparison is made of and what it cannot carry.

### 10.1 What the table is built from

This volume's corpus holds two sources that compare modalities across physical types. One compares TMS, transcranial electrical stimulation (tES), transcranial ultrasonic stimulation (TUS) and transcranial photobiomodulation (tPBM) on principles, clinical use and dosimetry, and discusses depth–focality trade-offs across coil and electrode designs [30]. The other spans the non-invasive to surgical range — TMS, tDCS and TUS through to deep brain stimulation (DBS), spinal cord stimulation (SCS) and vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) — and notes that many of the therapies it lists are standard care [31]. A third classifies implantable electrical therapies for chronic pain by target and by waveform and dose, and records that published studies often omit the effective dose, which prevents replication [2]. Naming is unsettled even inside one family, where a tES nomenclature paper trades breadth against the specificity reproducibility needs [4].

<!-- synthesis -->
Every spatial and temporal figure in the table is as reported by that family's own literature, in that literature's own preparation and units. No two were produced by the same procedure. The TMS depth and spread figures are finite-element simulations in a spherical head model [56]. The tES field values are intracranial recordings in patients with implanted electrodes [40,211]. Ultrasound exposure is reported twice, as free-field output and as an in situ estimate [19,20]. What to extract from a field is not standardised either: 202 tES and TMS studies used 308 different E-field outcome measures, with region-of-interest and whole-brain percentile measures overlapping by about 6% within a person [14]. The head is a variable too: uncorrected imaging artefacts alone shifted local E-field estimates by more than 30% [12]. The table is therefore a map of what each literature says about itself, not a measurement of the families against one another.

<!-- synthesis -->
Where no source available to this section reports a quantity for a family, the cell reads "not established in this corpus". The table has 36 rows and 288 content cells, of which 22 are the placeholder alone and 33 contain the phrase. Only the trigeminal nerve stimulation row is still empty in five of eight cells [140]. The remaining gap sits in particular columns rather than in whole families: 15 of the 33 are in the "named rival" column and 5 in "proposed primary coupling mechanism", while the evidence-base column has none. Dose and outcome are reported for almost every family; a named alternative explanation is not [30,31].

### 10.2 The families that comparison tables omit

<!-- synthesis -->
Eleven of the listed families appear in neither of the corpus's cross-modality sources [30,31]: magnetic seizure therapy, epidural cortical stimulation as a therapy, intracortical microstimulation, transcranial random noise stimulation (tRNS), transcranial pulse stimulation, peripheral or organ-directed ultrasound, sensory entrainment, galvanic vestibular stimulation, transcranial static magnetic field stimulation, cortical cooling, and brain–computer interfaces with write-in stimulation. Most of them do have a literature of their own here — a randomised head-to-head against ECT [227], four-year implanted human series [250], 53 clinical trials of parameters [237], a first-in-human splenic study [259] — but none of it is comparative, so every row below was assembled from that family's own sources rather than lifted from a review that had already placed it on shared axes.

<!-- synthesis -->
Three families have almost nothing even so. tRNS has no mechanistic or parameter-specific source at all: it appears only inside a pooled tES safety envelope covering tDCS, tACS, tRNS and related methods together across more than 300,000 sessions, to 4 mA and 60 min/day [43]. Epidural cortical stimulation appears only as a recording method, in human recordings of descending corticospinal volleys during transcranial stimulation [58]. Of the cranial nerves, the trigeminal appears only as a recruitment control in a VNS parameter study, where pulse-locked trigeminal activity emerged above 1.2 mA in rat [140], while the hypoglossal and occipital nerves now each carry their own randomised trial [252,255] and are split out as separate rows.

<!-- synthesis -->
Two families remain thin for reasons other than a missing literature. Spinal cord stimulation rests on one computational model validated in rats and one expert taxonomy, with no human mechanism source [2,61]. Cortical cooling has no human therapeutic series, and the reason is a species gap in the drive itself: below 24 °C aborts seizures within seconds in vivo and about 20 °C terminates paroxysmal activity in vitro [242], while intraoperative surface cooling of human cortex achieved only about 0.6–2 °C at 10–15 mm depth [243]. Electroconvulsive therapy is no longer thin — it now carries a randomised parameter trial, a dosing analysis, a sham-controlled meta-analysis and a mechanism review [260,261,262,263] — but its central question is open: whether the antidepressant action comes from the seizure, from the electricity, or from their interaction [263]. That dispute is why the magnetic seizure therapy row matters beyond its own family: comparable efficacy in a blinded head-to-head [227], from a field simulation puts well inside ECT's suprathreshold volume [225], is the strongest available argument for the seizure.

<!-- synthesis -->
Two of the newly filled rows have a negative sham-controlled trial as their best evidence, in both cases postdating a positive uncontrolled result. For transcranial pulse stimulation, a double-blind sham-controlled crossover trial in 60 Alzheimer's patients found the primary CERAD score rising 2.22 points after verum against 1.00 after sham, with a non-significant condition-by-session interaction (P = .68) and benefit confined to a prespecified subgroup aged 70 or under [229]; the 35-patient open-label series it supersedes had reported improvement sustained to three months [228]. For sensory entrainment, a six-month trial in 70 analysable patients missed its primary cognitive composite while several unadjusted secondaries were nominally favourable [234], where pilot work in 15 patients had reported less ventricular dilation and hippocampal atrophy [233]. Sensory entrainment also carries a rival at the level of mechanism rather than of parameters: steady-state responses can be reproduced by shifting and summing rate-matched transient evoked responses, with no signature of entrainment at the stimulation frequency [232], against the resonance reading of the human flicker frequency-response curve, which peaks near 10, 20, 40 and 80 Hz [231].

### 10.3 The benchmark that sits outside the table

The table's rows are families applied in humans, which leaves out the engineered-actuator modalities that set the field's specificity standard: channelrhodopsin-2 delivered by lentivirus, giving millisecond control of spiking from one gene plus light [90]; designer receptors evolved to answer a supposedly inert ligand at signalling timescales [93]; and misexpressed channels conferring acoustic sensitivity in *C. elegans* [72] and in mouse layer V motor cortex through intact skull [73]. A comparative review puts the temporal contrast at about three orders of magnitude: ChR2 opens within about 1–2 ms and closes within 10–20 ms, whereas chemogenetic effects begin 15–30 min after systemic ligand and last about 6–10 h [32]. They are not exempt from the table's problems. Systemically injected clozapine-N-oxide does not enter the brain and has low designer-receptor affinity; it is converted to clozapine, which does [29]. A ferritin-based magnetic actuator was reported to control behaviour in zebrafish and mice [95], and a first-principles analysis put the forces and heating required five to ten orders of magnitude beyond what paramagnetic ferritin can supply [94].

<!-- synthesis -->
Read down the columns rather than across the rows and two patterns show. First, invasiveness does not track how well the coupling step is specified. The two best-specified coupling accounts belong at opposite ends of the invasiveness range — an implanted electrode's activating function, where the driving term is the second spatial derivative of extracellular potential along a fibre [33,34], and a drug's receptor occupancy window, measured in humans by PET [26,27]. The upper bound on spatial resolution is one electrode of an implanted array, where a single contact yields a single localised percept graded by amplitude [248,251]; the lower bound is a family whose selectivity comes from a sense organ rather than from a field [231]. The least-specified sit in the middle: low-intensity ultrasound has at least four live candidate transducers [65,66,69,71], tPBM has an unresolved disagreement about how much light arrives, between a measured maximum transmittance below 1% and a modelled 15% of incident energy, quantities the two papers define differently [22,23], and temporal interference has a modelled threshold roughly two orders of magnitude above what is reachable in a human head [48]. Second, every family with a transferable dose unit got it from a safety problem rather than from a mechanism: equivalent minutes at 43 °C for thermal exposure [85], charge density with charge per phase for implanted electrodes [16,17], and the mechanical index for ultrasound [19]. The convulsive families are the exception that shows the rule: their inherited units — charge, energy, multiples of seizure threshold — are argued to be the wrong abstraction altogether, because placement and pulse width change outcome at constant charge [260,261].

## 11. Drugs as neuromodulation

<!-- framing -->
Section 5 took receptor binding as one coupling route among five. This section takes the drug as a drive in its own right, with an exposure, a delivery geometry and a set of contested mechanisms, and asks what changes when pharmacology is filed as a peer of the devices.

### 11.1 Route is a first-class variable

Route fixes what a montage or a transducer placement fixes: onset, exposure profile over time, and compartment reached. The clearest demonstration changes only the route. In a randomised double-blind crossover study of 20 human patients with spinal spasticity from multiple sclerosis or spinal cord injury who had failed oral baclofen, intrathecal infusion of the same GABA-B agonist substantially lowered muscle tone in every patient and reduced spasms in 18 of 19 affected patients, with benefit maintained over a mean 19.2 months of pump infusion [163]. Oral dosing puts occupancy where the side effects are; intrathecal dosing puts it where the circuit is [163].

Timing is as route-dependent as location. A human review attributes levodopa's wearing-off, dyskinesia and long-duration response largely to erratic plasma uptake and dose-to-dose variability rather than to receptor pharmacology: pulsatile and continuous exposure of the same receptors are different drives [186].

Some routes target the drive spatially, as a device does. Convection-enhanced delivery replaces diffusion with bulk flow: in animal brain, a sustained pressure gradient during interstitial infusion into white matter carried solute centimetres rather than millimetres, with volume of distribution scaling linearly with volume infused — about 6:1 distribution-to-infusion for the large tracer $^{111}$In-transferrin and about 13:1 for $^{14}$C-sucrose — and reached local concentrations orders of magnitude above achievable systemic levels [160]. Its dose parameters are infusion rate, volume, catheter placement and tissue hydraulic conductivity [160].

The intranasal route is claimed to bypass the circulation. The mechanistic review sets out olfactory and trigeminal pathways with perineural and perivascular bulk flow as routes to CNS tissue that skip the systemic circulation [161]. A systematic appraisal of roughly 100 mostly animal studies found only twelve adequately designed to test direct transport, of which two, both in rats, indicated it; the authors report no pharmacokinetic evidence that intranasal dosing in humans delivers more drug to brain targets than intravenous dosing at comparable systemic exposure, and note that the human olfactory area is proportionally far smaller than the rodent's [162]. Their design criteria name the test: matched doses by the two routes, brain and plasma exposure measured in the same subjects [162]. A route can be decisive in practice while that claim stays open: TRANSFORM-2 randomised 227 patients with treatment-resistant depression to flexibly dosed intranasal esketamine or placebo spray, each with a new oral antidepressant, and found greater MADRS improvement by day 28 [169].

### 11.2 The barrier, and why a potent compound still fails

For a drug, the analogue of a device's field model is a target-site exposure model. CNS exposure is set by passive diffusion plus active influx and efflux at the barrier, then by distribution between plasma, brain extracellular fluid and CSF; the authoritative review adds that disease-related changes in barrier function rarely yield large gains in penetration, and that carrier-based strategies remain more promising for large molecules [164]. Plasma concentration is not the dose; concentration in brain extracellular fluid at the receptor is [164]. Focused ultrasound with microbubbles is one way round it [80,82].

Failure can also follow entry, because the compound that acts may not be the one given: PET and pharmacology in rodent and rhesus macaque showed that systemically injected clozapine-N-oxide does not enter the brain, and that the designer receptors it was built for are occupied by its metabolite clozapine instead [29]. Affinity measured in a dish predicts nothing until exposure at the target is measured [29,164].

### 11.3 The field's mechanism test case: rapid-acting antidepressants

The clinical result came first and was small: seven human patients with major depression each completed two randomised test days with intravenous ketamine 0.5 mg/kg or saline, and within 72 h of ketamine 25-item Hamilton scores fell by about 14 points against essentially no change after placebo [165]. The replication, in 18 patients with treatment-resistant major depression at the same 0.5 mg/kg intravenous dose, found improvement on the 21-item Hamilton scale within 110 minutes, persisting about a week, with 71% response and 29% remission at day 1 [166]. An effect outlasting clearance by days cannot be carried by continuing occupancy [166].

The leading molecular account is synaptogenic. In rats, ketamine rapidly activated mTOR signalling in prefrontal cortex, raised synaptic signalling proteins and increased the number and function of dendritic spines, and rapamycin blocked both the synaptogenesis and the antidepressant-like behaviour [115]. A partly competing rodent account routes the same drive through eEF2 kinase deactivation and rapid BDNF translation [116].

Which molecular species acts is separately disputed. In mice, the metabolite (2R,6R)-hydroxynorketamine produced antidepressant-like effects independent of NMDA receptor inhibition but dependent on AMPA receptor activation, without ketamine's side-effect profile [117]. A target-deconvolution study then screened binding and function against thousands of human proteins and found that (2R,6R)-HNK binds none of the known or proposed ketamine targets and identified no other direct protein interaction, while the compound still showed antidepressant-like behavioural activity; those authors leave efficacy to clinical trials [167]. The counter-move keeps the metabolite but denies it needs a target of its own: in male mice, ketamine showed an inverted-U dose-response, effective at 10 mg/kg and ineffective higher, and pretreatment with MK-801 or CPP abolished its behavioural effect and also that of (2R,6R)-HNK, a GABA-A modulator and an mGlu2/3 antagonist — read by the authors as a shared requirement for downstream NMDA receptor activation [168].

Nitrous oxide adds a dose dissociation: in 24 human patients with severe treatment-resistant depression, three single one-hour inhalations — 50% nitrous oxide, 25% nitrous oxide and placebo air-oxygen — improved HDRS-21 scores versus placebo ($P=0.01$) with no difference between concentrations ($P=0.58$); separation emerged at weeks 1 and 2, not at 2 or 24 hours, and adverse events fell sharply at 25% ($P<0.001$) [185].

<!-- synthesis -->
The ketamine dispute has the shape of the device disputes in sections 4 and 5: rival mechanisms, each strongest in a different preparation, the human data unable to discriminate. Naming an active species is not bookkeeping, because the nominal compound, a metabolite with no identified receptor, and a downstream requirement shared with structurally unrelated drugs are three different edges from drive to molecular endpoint [115,116,117,167,168]. The chronic monoaminergic drugs make the same point about levels: one review locates the delay to clinical onset in the middle links, at altered emotional processing and drug-induced plasticity rather than at receptor occupancy [187], while an umbrella review of 17 systematic reviews, meta-analyses and large cohort or genetic studies searched to December 2020 — including gene association studies with $n=115{,}257$ and $n=43{,}165$ finding no transporter-genotype by stress interaction — concludes that the main research areas give no support to depression being caused by lowered serotonin; replies in the same journal contest its methods [188].

### 11.4 Psychedelics: compartment, locus, and the experience

The standard chain runs from 5-HT2A agonism at layer V pyramidal apical dendrites to a network effect: human imaging finds reduced oscillatory power in default-mode network regions after psilocybin or LSD [172]. Section 5 gave the complication: the cortical plasticity-promoting effects were attributed to intracellular rather than cell-surface 5-HT2A receptors in rodent and in vitro work [92].

The locus is contested a second way. Using CRISPR-engineered knockout mice and slice physiology, one group found long-lasting psychedelic-induced synaptic strengthening in granular retrosplenial cortex, whose neurons lack postsynaptic 5-HT2A receptors, and showed the effect required presynaptic 5-HT2A receptors on anterior thalamic axons projecting into it [174]. A receptor-expression map is not a map of where the drug acts [174]. The current mechanism review separates the necessity questions — 5-HT2A signalling for the hallucinogenic effect, and for the plasticity and antidepressant effects — assessing the second critically [173].

That dissociation is what makes the clinical exchange live. In a phase 2 trial, 104 adults with major depressive disorder received a single 25 mg dose of psilocybin or 100 mg niacin as active placebo, both with psychological support, and psilocybin gave a 12.3-point greater MADRS reduction to day 43 ($P<.001$) [177]. One position holds that with overall drug intensity statistically controlled, specific subjective features predict therapeutic outcome across human studies, making the neurobiological mechanisms necessary but not sufficient [175]. The opposing paper in the same exchange argues that link is largely correlational, presents data suggesting the subjective effects are not required for lasting change, and frames the question as decisive for non-hallucinogenic analogues [176]. Both sides are undercut by one measurement problem: a systematic search of psychedelic and ketamine RCTs found these trials generally do not report pre-trial expectancy or blinding success, and argues that because the acute effects are unmistakable, participants are effectively unblinded and effect sizes overestimates [178].

### 11.5 Neurosteroids and anaesthetics: the reversible, fast end

Neurosteroids are the template for a drug as a gain change rather than an input: certain pregnane steroids potently and selectively enhance GABA-A receptor function by a direct, non-genomic action, and act both as circulating messengers and as locally synthesised modulators, so one molecule can be a systemic or a local drive [179]. The clinical descendants differ in route and schedule at fixed pharmacology. Brexanolone, an intravenous formulation of allopregnanolone, was given as a single 60-hour infusion in two phase 3 trials in women with moderate to severe postpartum depression, with greater Hamilton reduction than placebo at 60 h (study 2: 14.6 versus 12.1 points) [180]; oral zuranolone 50 mg once daily for 14 days in 196 women with severe postpartum depression gave a least-squares mean difference of −4.0 on the 17-item Hamilton at day 15 (95% CI −6.3 to −1.7), maintained to day 45 [181]. A Cochrane review of five studies in 349 women confirms the distinct mechanism but finds the evidence limited — one trial showing improved patient-reported maternal functioning at day 45 at low certainty, none assessing quality of life — and judges it insufficient to recommend these drugs over existing treatments [182].

Anaesthesia is the most complete reversible whole-brain case: chemically diverse anaesthetics act at a small number of molecular targets, and the path to reversible loss of consciousness runs through the endogenous sleep and arousal circuitry, with the thalamus central [183].

### 11.6 Drug plus device

Pharmacological challenge is how a receptor identity gets assigned to a stimulation-induced plasticity effect, which makes drug-plus-device studies measurement instruments first [189]. The adjunct trial is the sharpest version: 50 participants with major depressive disorder received four weeks of intermittent theta-burst stimulation with D-cycloserine 100 mg, an NMDA receptor partial agonist, for the first two weeks, or placebo, and the D-cycloserine arm showed a 6.15-point greater MADRS reduction (Hedges $g=0.99$), with response 73.9% versus 29.3% and remission 39.1% versus 4.2% [190].

Receptor dependence does not generalise to every neuromodulator system. Rats given VNS paired with a jaw-movement behaviour while dosed daily with oxybutynin, prazosin, duloxetine or saline showed the expected VNS-dependent expansion of the jaw representation on intracortical microstimulation mapping in the saline group; none of the three drugs at clinically relevant doses blocked it [142].

Head-to-head, drug and device are commensurable, and the answer is unsettled. An open-label randomised non-inferiority trial in 403 patients compared three weeks of ECT three times weekly with intravenous ketamine 0.5 mg/kg twice weekly, and found QIDS-SR-16 response of 55.4% with ketamine against 41.2% with ECT, meeting non-inferiority, with early memory decline after ECT that recovered later [170]. A comparable open-label design in hospitalised patients with unipolar depression, using thrice-weekly racemic ketamine, found remission of 63% with ECT against 46% with ketamine, so non-inferiority was not established; both arms needed a median of six sessions [171]. The two differ in setting, severity and endpoint — outpatient against hospitalised, self-reported response against remission [170,171].

### 11.7 What this level implies for a graph

<!-- synthesis -->
Three requirements follow. First, route, exposure profile and compartment reached are properties of the drive node rather than annotations on it: the same molecule given orally and intrathecally [163], or by 60-hour infusion and by 14-day oral course [180,181], is a different drive each time, and its exposure is modelled at the target site rather than read from plasma [164]. Second, the molecular endpoint node is shared with the device branches, which makes the adjunct result representable: a drug acting on the receptor that gates stimulation-induced plasticity composes with the device at that node [189,190], while a failed blockade shows the composition is system-specific [142]. Third, every drug edge must name its active species and its receptor's compartment, because the administered compound is sometimes not the acting one [29], the proposed active metabolite may have no identified target [167], and the same agonist acts differently inside the cell and at the surface [92]. The closest precedent is a curated database of drug mechanisms as paths through a biomedical knowledge graph, covering 4,583 drug-indication pairs and 32,249 relationships [223]; it holds the path but not the route, the exposure or the dispute.

## 12. How the work is done, and how it fools you

<!-- framing -->
This section is meant to be used as a checklist: each subsection ends with the questions to put to a new paper, and the last turns them into graph rules.

### 12.1 The anatomy of a stimulation experiment

A stimulation study makes a causal claim along a chain: the induced field acts on local tissue, the local effect spreads through a network, and the network change alters behaviour. Bergmann and Hartwigsen set this chain out step by step and list the confounds that weaken a causal claim at each step [199]. In this book's terms, a study that measures only drive and outcome leaves coupling and circuit inferred [199].

What is manipulated is a parameter set, not an intensity. In human motor cortex, epidural recordings of descending volleys show that TMS intensity and current direction select different excitatory and inhibitory circuits [58]. In rat thalamus, suppression of evoked potentials by focused ultrasound tracked measured temperature and not duty cycle, peak pressure or modulation frequency [71], and in ex vivo salamander retina standing waves shaped the effective stimulus [66].

What is measured is usually a proxy several levels from the coupling step: a motor evoked potential, an EEG deflection, a pupil diameter, a symptom score (12.4). What is controlled is usually sensation, through a sham, and rarely site or timing. Sham TMS can have specific behavioural effects of its own, so it belongs alongside active controls at another site or time rather than instead of them [198].

The degrees of freedom hide in the gaps between these three. Brain state is one. In human motor cortex, the same triple-pulse TMS bursts produced LTP-like potentiation of motor evoked potentials when locked to the negative peak of the mu rhythm and no change when locked to the positive peak [137]. TMS-EEG measures did not show a matching change, and cross-lab replication is thin [137]. Target definition is another. In 295 rTMS patients, connectivity between the stimulated site and subgenual cingulate predicted outcome only weakly (r about −0.16), and only when the site was defined by electric-field modelling rather than nominal coil position [130].

**Checklist.** Is the full parameter set reported, including envelope, geometry and temperature? Was brain state controlled or recorded? Was the target defined by where the field went or by where the coil sat? Is there an active control for site or timing as well as a sham [71,130,137,198]?

### 12.2 Sham and blinding

A sham is a procedure, and different procedures deliver different things. The sham tDCS protocols in use differ in more than sensation, so some may have biological effects of their own, which makes the choice of sham a source of irreproducibility [195]. A review of sham tDCS protocols from 2010 to 2024 says the same of ramp-only shams, which ramp current up and down briefly: they may be neither physiologically inert nor perceptually convincing during the session [196]. Whether short ramp shams are inert remains contested [195,196].

Whether blinding worked is usually judged from guesses. A meta-analysis of 7 sham-controlled rTMS trials for depression (n = 489) found that participants' guesses about allocation did not differ between active and sham arms, and judged blinding adequate [197]. That review disputes the method: checks at the end of a study overestimate how well masking worked, while checks during stimulation reveal perceptual differences [196].

The same problem is larger in pharmacology, where the acute effect is unmistakable. A systematic search of psychedelic and ketamine trials from 1990 to November 2020 found that they generally reported neither pre-trial expectancy nor blinding success; the authors argue participants are effectively unblinded and effect sizes are likely overestimates [178]. One response is an active placebo, as in the psilocybin trial that used 100 mg niacin [177].

<!-- synthesis -->
The guess-questionnaire dispute would be settled by trials that probe blinding during the session, before outcomes are known, and report both in-session and end-of-study guesses [196,197].

**Checklist.** Which sham, with what ramp, electrode or coil arrangement? Was blinding checked during the session or only at the end? Were expectancy and guesses reported by arm? Does the active arm have a percept sham lacks [178,195–198]?

### 12.3 Co-stimulation as a rival explanation

Every non-invasive drive also stimulates the scalp, the ear or peripheral nerves; the TMS consensus paper states that TMS causes substantial somatosensory and auditory co-stimulation that must be controlled for [41]. The question is never whether the side-channel exists, but whether it alone produces the headline effect.

**Ultrasound and hearing (contested).** In guinea pigs, widespread cortical and subcortical activation by transcranial ultrasound disappeared when the auditory nerves were cut or cochlear fluid removed [74]. In mice, wide-field calcium imaging showed ultrasound-evoked cortical activity resembling the response to audible sound; both produced startle-like motor responses, and chemical deafening reduced both [75]. The rebuttal also came from mice: sharp-edged rectangular pulses did activate the auditory pathway, but smoothing the pulse envelope removed the auditory brainstem response and left the EMG motor response intact, and genetically deaf mice responded like hearing mice [76]. On that account the confound is a pulse-edge transient that can be engineered away [76].

The strongest human evidence points the other way. Four experiments at three institutions, one preregistered, tracked TMS-evoked motor potentials during ultrasound pulsed at 1000 Hz [77]. The reported motor inhibition replicated, but manipulating intensity, duration and auditory masking showed it was driven by peripheral auditory stimulation, and the authors argue earlier work using only a flip-over sham needs reinterpretation [77]. A preregistered follow-up in 25 participants characterised a second channel, tactile, thermal and painful scalp sensation, and mitigated it by avoiding near-field scalp intensity peaks, spreading energy, ramping pulses and using longer, lower-intensity pulses [78].

**Transcranial alternating current and the skin (contested).** In rats and humans, Asamoah and colleagues argued that cortical tACS fields are too weak for direct activation while skin fields are much stronger; anaesthetising the skin reduced tACS-induced tremor entrainment, and peripheral nerve stimulation alone reproduced the motor effects [54]. In macaque hippocampus and visual cortex, neurons stayed entrained to tACS after topical anaesthesia blocked somatosensory input [55].

<!-- synthesis -->
The two tACS results need not conflict: a peripheral route can produce a human motor effect and a direct route can entrain macaque neurons [54,55]. Either way, the test for a given claim is to block or match the peripheral input and see what survives [54,55,74,76,77].

The logic reaches beyond devices: systemic clozapine N-oxide does not enter the brain but is converted to clozapine, which does, and which occupies the designer receptors [29]. A route that bypasses the assumed mechanism is the chemical form of the same problem.

**Checklist.** For ultrasound: was the sham acoustically matched rather than transducer-off or flipped, was the pulse envelope ramped, was masking used, and were scalp sensations measured [76–78]? For tES: was skin sensation controlled or anaesthetised [54,55]? For any drug: was brain entry of the administered compound shown, or only assumed [29]?

### 12.4 Readouts and their validity

The motor evoked potential (MEP) is the workhorse readout; the IFCN procedural reference defines its standard measures, motor threshold, input-output curves and paired-pulse protocols [200]. Drug challenge is the main tool for giving such a measure a receptor identity [189].

**How much of the TMS-evoked potential is transcranial (contested).** A realistic sham mimicking the sound and scalp sensation of TMS evoked EEG responses closely resembling those to real TMS at early and late latencies, despite noise masking and foam padding [202]. With an optimised sham, noise masking, a second coil stacked on the real one and electrical scalp stimulation to saturate touch, subtraction left early TEP components and beta-band responses that occurred only with real TMS [203]. The 2023 TMS-EEG consensus lists peripheral sensory contamination as an open problem [201]. The dispute turns on whether a sham can match real TMS closely enough for subtraction to be valid [202,203].

Proxies further from the target need validation of their own. In mice, pupil dilation rose monotonically with locus coeruleus spike count but predicted only a small fraction of moment-to-moment LC activity, and was itself state-dependent [144].

**Checklist.** How many levels lie between the readout and the claim? For TMS-EEG, was a multisensory sham recorded and how were components separated [201–203]? For a proxy biomarker, is there evidence linking it to the quantity it indexes, and how tight is that link [144]?

### 12.5 Target engagement before efficacy

An efficacy trial tests the whole chain at once, so a null cannot say which link failed; target verification means showing the local and network steps too [199].

Transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation (taVNS) shows why. In 66 healthy adults, continuous cymba conchae stimulation just below pain threshold left pupil dilation, cortisol and salivary alpha-amylase unchanged against earlobe sham [145]. A living Bayesian meta-analysis of 18 studies (N = 771) found anecdotal evidence overall (g = 0.15, BF01 = 1.0), strong evidence for an effect with pulsed stimulation (g = 0.36, BF10 = 50.8), and strong evidence for the null with continuous stimulation (g = 0.002, BF01 = 21.9) [146]. On this evidence, engagement depends on the waveform [146].

Engagement evidence differs by modality; the table covers this corpus only.

| Modality | Engagement evidence | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| TMS | MEP measures; TEPs after multisensory sham [200,203] | TEP sensory share disputed [202] |
| rTMS targeting | Site–sgACC connectivity with E-field-defined targets [130] | About 3% of outcome variance [130] |
| tES | Concurrent tES–fMRI, scored on the ContES items [194] | 57 studies met about 53% of items [194] |

**Checklist.** Did the study, or a prior one with the same parameters, show that the drive reached and changed its target before claiming an outcome [145,146,199]?

### 12.6 Variability and reproducibility

Most people do not show the textbook response. In 56 healthy adults given paired associative stimulation, anodal tDCS and iTBS, only 39%, 45% and 43% responded in the expected direction, and response to one protocol did not predict response to another [204]. Theta-burst stimulation was introduced as producing reproducible excitability changes from 20–190 s of stimulation [38]; in a sample of 56 adults its after-effects varied widely instead, tracking late I-wave recruitment as indexed by MEP latency with anterior-posterior current [59].

Within one person, responses repeat poorly: across 15 studies (291 participants) of motor-cortex rTMS after-effects, test-retest reliability was r 0.10–0.55 and ICC 0.29–0.70, with no protocol significantly more reliable than another [205]. In a survey of electrical brain stimulation researchers, only about 45–50% said they could routinely reproduce published results, and an audit found the rigorous practices they reported were rarely documented in papers [206]. Low power compounds this: it lowers the chance of detecting true effects, lowers the chance that a significant result is true, and inflates effect sizes [207].

**Checklist.** Were responders pre-defined or found after the fact? Is the sample size justified by a power analysis? Was the study preregistered, and are individual data reported [204–207]?

### 12.7 Reporting standards

Checklists now exist for the main electrical designs. A 2017 tDCS consensus proposed a reporting checklist plus double-blinding, preregistration, data sharing and publication of nulls [192]. A three-round Delphi study with 38 experts produced RATES: 66 items in five groups (participants, stimulator, electrodes, current, procedure), with a 26-item essential version [193]. For concurrent tES-fMRI, a two-phase Delphi process produced 17 essential items and 42 further recommendations; applied to 57 published studies, they met about 53% of items on average [194].

For ultrasound, the inTUS repository of parameters and outcomes acts as a de facto schema; its authors name incomplete parameter reporting and selection bias as limits on their own tentative meta-analysis of 32 studies [79]. The TMS-EEG recommendations cover recording, artefact correction and interpretation [201].

<!-- synthesis -->
What stays unreported in practice, on this evidence, is what checklists cannot force: in-session blinding checks [196], expectancy in drug trials [178], scalp and auditory sensation in ultrasound [77,78], thermal measurement [71], and documentation of the practices researchers say they follow [206].

**Checklist.** Against which checklist was the study reported, and which essential items are missing [192–194,201]?

### 12.8 What this implies for a graph

<!-- synthesis -->
Three schema rules follow. First, every edge carries a record of its confound controls: sham type, when blinding was checked, masking, sensory matching, and whether engagement was shown [77,196,199]. Second, a confound is a node with edges to the claims it threatens (auditory input to ultrasound inhibition [77], skin input to tACS motor effects [54], sensory input to TEPs [202]), and rebuttals are counter-edges on that node [55,76,203]. Third, a claim from an unblinded or sham-inadequate study carries a flag, so it can be filtered or down-weighted and is never merged with a controlled one [178,196].

<!-- synthesis -->
Pooled estimates need the same treatment: a human ultrasound meta-analysis [79] inherits whatever confound its inputs carried [77], so the graph should record that dependency, not only the pooled number.

## 13. Evidence, species and closed loops

<!-- framing -->
Section 12 asked how a single study can fool you. This section asks what happens at the joins: guideline to mechanism, rodent to human, preparation to patient, fixed dose to controlled dose.

### 13.1 What a recommendation is, and what it is not

The general framework is GRADE, introduced because guideline developers graded evidence inconsistently. It rates two things separately: the certainty of the evidence for an effect, and the strength of the recommendation that follows from it [208].

Neuromodulation mostly does not use GRADE. The European rTMS guideline grades each indication against sham on a letter scale, where level A means definite efficacy: high-frequency rTMS of M1 for neuropathic pain, high-frequency rTMS of left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex for depression, and low-frequency rTMS for hand motor recovery after stroke. Level B, the next level down, covers Parkinson's disease, fibromyalgia, spasticity in multiple sclerosis and PTSD [209]. The tDCS guideline rates tDCS definitely effective for depression and neuropathic pain and probably or possibly effective elsewhere, with small to moderate effect sizes and benefits that often did not last [210].

<!-- synthesis -->
A level A is therefore a statement about one edge: drive to outcome, in one indication, against sham, with the protocol fixed. It says nothing about the coupling, cellular or molecular steps, and does not license any mechanistic story about why the outcome moved [208–210]. A graph that reads the letter as confidence in a mechanism has mistranslated it.

Two further gaps sit inside "the evidence says". Approval and graded certainty are different thresholds: a Cochrane review of neurosteroid GABA-A positive allosteric modulators for postnatal depression (five studies, 349 women) found only low-certainty benefit and insufficient evidence to recommend them over existing treatments [182]. And a drug can work while its putative target deficit fails to appear: an umbrella review found no association between serotonin metabolite concentrations and depression, and no serotonin-transporter-genotype by stress interaction in studies of 115,257 and 43,165 people [188].

Even the efficacy edge is not one number. ELEKT-D, in 403 outpatients, found 55.4% response with ketamine against 41.2% with ECT and met non-inferiority [170]; KetECT, in hospitalised patients, found 63% remission with ECT against 46% with ketamine and did not [171]. Setting and endpoint differ, so the answer is not a property of the two modalities alone [170,171].

Across modalities the spread is wide. Human transcranial temporal interference stimulation has 20 completed studies and 28 ongoing trials (820 participants), which a systematic review calls Phase 1 evidence with efficacy untested [47]. Focused ultrasound thalamotomy for essential tremor beat a sham procedure in a randomised trial: hand-tremor scores fell from 18.1 to 9.6 at three months against 16.0 to 15.8 with sham [86].

### 13.2 Species translation: fields do not scale with heads

Intracranial recordings during transcranial electric stimulation in non-human primates and human epilepsy patients measured peak fields of about 0.5 mV/mm in superficial cortex, changing little with frequency or region [211]. Finite-element models of mouse, monkey and human heads predict that TMS field strength first rises and then falls with head size, and that tES fields differ by up to about 100-fold across the three species; the authors propose matching animal parameters to human fields rather than copying them [212].

<!-- synthesis -->
The consequence for rodent mechanism work is specific. An experiment run at fields a human montage cannot produce still shows that a coupling mechanism exists; it does not show that the human protocol engages it. What transfers is the physics of the coupling step; what does not is the number [211,212].

The readout may not transfer even when the field does. Translocator protein (TSPO) rose in activated microglia in mouse models but not in non-human primates or human neurodegenerative disease, a gap traced to a promoter AP1 site, so human TSPO-PET probably reflects the density of inflammatory cells rather than their activation [127]. A stimulation claim about neuroinflammation graded on TSPO imaging inherits that mismatch.

Pharmacology meets the same problem at the delivery interface. Of roughly 100 mostly animal studies of direct nose-to-brain transport, twelve were adequately designed to test it and two, in rats, supported it; there was no human pharmacokinetic evidence for it, and the human olfactory area is proportionally far smaller than the rodent's [162]. For drugs the analogue of a field model is a target-site exposure model, because plasma concentration is not the concentration at the receptor [164].

### 13.3 Preparation translation, and which way it bends

Acoustic blood-brain barrier opening climbed from rat to human. Feedback-controlled sonication in 27 rats opened the barrier at a mean peak pressure of 0.28 ± 0.05 MPa [81], and a phase I trial in five patients with Alzheimer's disease then opened it safely, reversibly and repeatedly [82]. What did not climb was the therapeutic claim: amyloid PET showed no group-level change after sonication [82]. A rodent dispute also stays open (contested). One group reports that microbubble-assisted sonication causes a sterile inflammatory response lasting about 24 hours [83]; another finds the NF-κB response is set by microbubble dose and absent at clinical imaging doses [84]. Five patients cannot settle this; dose-graded inflammatory markers in humans would [82–84].

The neuroimmune chain is weakest at the human rung. A critical review accepts that stimulating the cut vagus suppresses inflammation in rodents and that the effect needs splenic noradrenergic terminals and α7 nicotinic receptors, but rejects the vagus-to-splenic-nerve link and so the reflex framing [151]. Human evidence is mostly correlational [152], and the pivotal trial in 242 patients with rheumatoid arthritis gave ACR20 responses of 35.2% with active stimulation and 24.2% with sham at three months [153].

<!-- synthesis -->
Where this corpus supports a direction of bias, effects shrink as controls tighten and samples grow. Low power inflates published effect sizes [207]; only about 45–50% of surveyed electrical-stimulation researchers could routinely reproduce published results [206]; unmeasured unblinding is argued to inflate drug-trial effects [178]. No study here measures an animal-to-human effect-size ratio directly, so the direction is an inference, not a measurement [178,206,207].

### 13.4 Closed loops: when the drive depends on the state it modulates

Feedback control began as a safety device. During 551.5 kHz sonication in 10 ms bursts at 2 Hz, a hydrophone listened for the microbubbles' ultraharmonic emissions; pressure was ramped until they appeared and then held at a set fraction of that threshold. At 50% the barrier opened with no detectable damage, and higher fractions caused gross damage [81].

In the brain the control variable is neural. Subthalamic beta-band local field potentials gated stimulation in eight patients with advanced Parkinson's disease. Blinded motor scores improved 50%, about 27% more than with continuous stimulation, while stimulation time fell by 56%; random intermittent stimulation did not match this, so timing, not intermittency, carried the benefit [135].

A blinded randomised cross-over feasibility trial in four patients then ran adaptive stimulation chronically at home. Its control signals were stimulation-entrained gamma oscillations in subthalamic nucleus or motor cortex, chosen per patient, and it improved motor symptoms and quality of life relative to optimised continuous stimulation [136].

<!-- synthesis -->
Two things about dose change here. Delivered charge falls as effect rises, so intensity stops indexing dose [135]. And because the threshold or marker is set per site or per patient, the delivered parameters form a distribution over states rather than a setting in a protocol table [81,135,136]. The biomarker also stops being a neutral observer: a control signal entrained by stimulation is partly produced by the intervention it governs [136].

Timing is part of dose even without a loop: in a meta-analysis of 16 randomised trials of prefrontal TMS or tDCS in healthy adults, the reduction in acute cortisol response to a laboratory stressor (SMD = −0.72) was larger when stimulation came before or after the stressor than during it [159].

The null result matters. Burst rTMS triggered at a mu-rhythm phase that raises motor evoked potentials in other work produced no detectable change in resting EEG or TMS-EEG excitability measures, and the authors conclude that EEG measures do not track corticospinal excitability [213]. Whether phase-triggered TMS effects generalise beyond the MEP is contested on this point [213]; the same protocol scored on MEP and EEG readouts in one sample would settle it.

### 13.5 What this level implies for a graph

<!-- synthesis -->
First, every edge carries an evidence tier in two fields: a GRADE-style certainty and an evidence type from a vocabulary such as ECO, so "level A for depression" and "measured in 27 rats" stay distinguishable [208,218]. Second, species and preparation are required on every mechanism edge, and for field-based drives so is the measured or modelled field in that species [211,212].

<!-- synthesis -->
Third, clinical-efficacy edges and mechanism edges are separate types. An efficacy edge needs indication, comparator, endpoint and setting [170,171]; approval is not certainty [182]; and a mechanism of disease is not inferred from a mechanism of drug [188]. The marker used to grade an edge also needs its own species validity [127,213].

<!-- synthesis -->
Fourth, each dose node records whether its parameters were fixed or feedback-controlled. If controlled, it records the control variable, the threshold rule, whether the variable was endogenous or stimulation-entrained, and the delivered distribution rather than a single value [81,135,136].

## 14. What is actually in dispute

<!-- framing -->
Sections 4 to 13 argued the mechanisms. This is the catalogue: one entry per live dispute, in a fixed shape, pointing back rather than re-explaining. The identifiers are the ones the graph will carry.


### 14.1 Transcranial field entrainment (H-tes-entrainment)

**The question** — Do 1–2 mA transcranial currents modulate cortical neurons directly?

**The positions** — *Direct*: tACS entrains weakly locked macaque units and desynchronises strongly locked ones [44], and does so through topical scalp anaesthesia [55]. *Peripheral, or too weak*: rat recordings put the minimum effective field near 1 mV/mm [50], and skin anaesthesia removed tACS tremor entrainment that peripheral nerve stimulation reproduced [54].

**What the evidence turns on** — delivered field against threshold, modulating ongoing firing needing less than exciting a silent cell, as first measured in invertebrate neurons [35]: 2 mA gave about 0.8 V/m intracranially in 10 human patients [40]. Section 4.5.

<!-- synthesis -->
**What would settle it** — intracranial recording in one human with and without scalp anaesthesia, the measured field reported beside the effect [40,50,54].

### 14.2 Temporal interference thresholds in humans (H-ti-suprathreshold)

**The question** — Does temporal interference reach neural thresholds in the human brain?

**The positions** — *Envelope neuromodulation*: anaesthetised mice followed the envelope [39]; human TI changed hippocampal fMRI activity and memory accuracy [45]. *Unreachable*: in layer-5 pyramidal models suprathreshold TI needed about 75–230 V/m against the 0.1–1 V/m that tolerated scalp currents deliver [48], and passive filtering cannot demodulate the envelope [60].

**What the evidence turns on** — suprathreshold firing against subthreshold timing: in macaques TI-tACS shifted spike timing, not rate [46]; human efficacy is untested [47]. Section 4.6.

<!-- synthesis -->
**What would settle it** — intracranial human recording at tolerated currents, rate and timing reported separately; a timing-only effect reconciles all four [45,46,48,60].

### 14.3 The ultrasound transducer (H-tus-mechanism)

**The question** — What converts a pressure wave into a change in neural activity?

**The positions** — *Intramembrane cavitation*: in a bilayer-sonophore model, leaflets separate under acoustic drive, rectifying pressure into a capacitance change [65]. *Radiation force*: salamander retinal responses grew from 0.5 to 43 MHz, opposite to the cavitation prediction [66]. *Mechanosensitive channels*: Piezo1 knockout in mouse cortex cut evoked calcium and EMG [68]; CFTR carried ultrasound-evoked chloride current in cells [70]. *Heat*: rat thalamic suppression tracked temperature, not duty cycle [71].

**What the evidence turns on** — frequency and parameter dependence, where temperature is measured rather than assumed [66,71]. Section 5.2.

<!-- synthesis -->
**What would settle it** — one preparation, a factorial sweep of frequency, duty cycle and pressure with thermometry, in knockout and wild-type tissue [65,68,71].

### 14.4 The ultrasound auditory confound (H-tus-confound)

**The question** — Are reported ultrasound effects produced at the focus or through the ear?

**The positions** — *Auditory-driven*: guinea-pig activation was abolished by auditory nerve transection [74]; mouse imaging matched auditory pathway activation and fell with deafening [75]; in four human experiments the motor inhibition tracked auditory stimulation [77]. *Genuine*: envelope smoothing removed the auditory brainstem response but left the mouse motor response intact, and deaf mice matched hearing ones [76].

**What the evidence turns on** — whether sham was acoustically matched rather than transducer-off, and whether pulse-edge transients were engineered away [79]. Section 12.3.

<!-- synthesis -->
**What would settle it** — human studies with acoustically matched sham, masked and unmasked arms, hearing and deaf participants, envelope reported as dose [74,76,77].

### 14.5 Barrier opening and sterile inflammation (H-fus-bbb-inflammation)

**The question** — Is focused-ultrasound blood–brain-barrier opening an inflammatory injury?

**The positions** — *An inflammatory event*: in rats, pulsed focused ultrasound with systemic microbubbles produced immediate damage-associated molecular patterns and a parenchymal sterile inflammatory response lasting about 24 hours, with a profile the authors compare to ischaemia or mild traumatic brain injury [83]. *A dose effect*: across three sonication protocols in rats, NF-κB pathway activation tracked microbubble dose — absent at clinical imaging doses, and accompanied by oedema, neuronal degeneration and microhaemorrhage at high doses [84].

**What the evidence turns on** — whether microbubble dose and exposure were reported at all, and whether the read-out is pathway gene expression or histology [83,84]. Section 5.3.

<!-- synthesis -->
**What would settle it** — a dose-ranging study reporting microbubble dose, acoustic parameters and inflammatory markers together; that moves the question from whether barrier opening inflames the brain to at what dose [83,84].

### 14.6 Photobiomodulation delivered dose (H-pbm-dose)

**The question** — Does enough near-infrared light reach cortex for transcranial photobiomodulation?

**The positions** — *Insufficient*: through human skull, maximum transmittance was 0.31% for a 905 nm laser and 0.71% for an 810 nm LED helmet, and mitochondrial stimulation in cells needed 14–775 times that level [22]. *Sufficient*: Monte Carlo photon-transport simulation at 810 and 1064 nm put up to about 15% of incident energy at cortex transcranially and about 1% intranasally, most of it deposited in ventromedial prefrontal cortex, with darker skin substantially reducing brain dose [23].

**What the evidence turns on** — measurement against simulation, and quantities that are not directly comparable: transmittance through a skull [22] is not the fraction of incident energy deposited in tissue [23]. Section 5.5.

<!-- synthesis -->
**What would settle it** — in-situ transmittance during a device protocol, reported in the same units as the simulation and against a dose–response curve for the endpoint claimed; the two sides may not yet disagree about anything measurable [22,23].

### 14.7 40 Hz sensory stimulation (H-gamma-40hz)

**The question** — Does 40 Hz sensory stimulation entrain gamma and reduce amyloid?

**The positions** — *Yes*: in Alzheimer's model mice, optogenetic 40 Hz driving of fast-spiking interneurons lowered Aβ1-40 and Aβ1-42 [123], and audiovisual 40 Hz raised CSF influx, amyloid removal failing when glymphatic clearance was blocked [124]. *No*: large-scale recordings in model mice found no native gamma engagement, flicker avoidance, and no plaque change [125].

**What the evidence turns on** — whether entrainment is scored as a driven evoked response or as engagement of the native rhythm [123,125]. Section 7.

<!-- synthesis -->
**What would settle it** — a pre-registered multi-site replication in one mouse line; the clearance claim survives the entrainment null if the route is vasomotor [124,125].

### 14.8 The tDCS after-effect account (H-tdcs-plasticity)

**The question** — Is the after-effect of tDCS cortical LTP-like plasticity?

**The positions** — *LTP-like*: sustained MEP change is read as associative plasticity, as in paired associative stimulation, where MEPs rose within 30 min and lasted 30–60 min [97,100]. *Peripheral*: six tDCS results are re-read as cranial and cervical nerve co-stimulation [109]. *Unreliable*: 430 participants across 22 theta-burst studies varied widely [107], and 59 studies found no single-session cognitive effect [108]. *Glial*: in mice the effect required astrocytic calcium [121].

**What the evidence turns on** — whether the read-out is synaptic or a corticospinal proxy, and whether the peripheral route was blocked [100,109]. Section 6.5.

<!-- synthesis -->
**What would settle it** — hold the cortical field constant while blocking scalp afferents, with a read-out other than the MEP [107,121].

### 14.9 Connectivity-based targeting (H-connectivity-targeting)

**The question** — Does subgenual cingulate connectivity predict rTMS outcome well enough to target by?

**The positions** — *Yes*: across 98 healthy subjects, DLPFC sites with better reported efficacy were more strongly anticorrelated with subgenual cingulate, efficacy taken from the literature [129]. *Too weak*: in 295 treated patients the association was about r = −0.16, some 3% of outcome variance, detectable only with electric-field-defined targets [130].

**What the evidence turns on** — retrospective site-level correlation against prospective patient-level prediction, and whether stimulated tissue was modelled [129,130]. Section 8.4.

<!-- synthesis -->
**What would settle it** — a randomised comparison of connectivity-derived against anatomical targets; a group gradient can coexist with a useless individual predictor [129,130].

### 14.10 Deep brain stimulation mechanism (H-dbs-mechanism)

**The question** — How does high-frequency deep brain stimulation produce a lesion-like effect?

**The positions** — *Informational lesion*: modelled high-frequency drive makes output spiking regular and almost variance-free, so the cell keeps firing but stops transmitting information, which reconciles the frequency requirement with the lesion-like picture [133]. *Excitation and antidromic activation*: that high-frequency stimulation drives efferent axons rather than silencing them [60].

**What the evidence turns on** — whether the quantity recorded is mean rate, spike-train variability or antidromic response; both poles here are computational [60,133]. Section 8.2.

<!-- synthesis -->
**What would settle it** — nucleus-output and cortical antidromic recording in patients at therapeutic and subtherapeutic frequencies, analysed as a pattern statistic [133].

### 14.11 The charge-density safety rule (H-charge-safety)

**The question** — Does the charge and charge-density rule bound safe stimulation?

**The positions** — *The Shannon line*: a model fitted to earlier tissue-damage data relates charge density and charge per phase to safe against damaging stimulation, and its log-linear boundary became the field's design rule for macroelectrodes [17]. *Out of scope*: a review of damage thresholds holds that the equation omits frequency, duty cycle, current density and electrode size, and that microelectrodes deliver higher charge densities without damage, so the thresholds need revising [18].

**What the evidence turns on** — whether the boundary is read as an empirical fit to macroelectrode damage data or as a general limit, and whether the electrode geometry in question was in that fit [17,18]. Section 3.

<!-- synthesis -->
**What would settle it** — damage thresholds measured across frequency, duty cycle and electrode size in one preparation; until then the rule is a fitted envelope, and the parameters it omits belong in the dose record rather than outside it [17,18].

### 14.12 The inflammatory-reflex framing (H-inflammatory-reflex)

**The question** — Is vagal control of splenic TNF a reflex arc, or a drivable pathway?

**The positions** — *A reflex*: the finding that cholinergic neurons inhibit acute inflammation was formalised as a hard-wired loop with an afferent limb, central integration and an efferent cholinergic limb, addressable electrically or pharmacologically [149]; the efferent arc was then completed by choline acetyltransferase-expressing splenic T cells, required in rodents for vagal stimulation to suppress TNF through α7 nicotinic receptors on macrophages [150]. *Drivable but not a reflex*: an audit accepts that stimulating the cut peripheral vagus suppresses inflammation and that splenic noradrenergic terminals, ChAT-positive T lymphocytes and α7 nicotinic receptors are required, but rejects the disynaptic vagus-to-splenic-nerve link, since vagal stimulation drives no splenic nerve spikes [151].

**What the evidence turns on** — splenic nerve recordings against downstream cytokine outcomes; the molecular chain [150] is not disputed, the wiring diagram and the word reflex are [149,151]. Section 9.

<!-- synthesis -->
**What would settle it** — splenic nerve recording during physiological endotoxaemia with and without vagotomy; only one framing licenses an edge labelled reflex, and the therapeutic claim survives either way [149,151].

### 14.13 Transcutaneous vagal target engagement (H-tavns-engagement)

**The question** — Does transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation engage noradrenergic targets in humans?

**The positions** — *Null*: in 66 healthy participants, continuous cymba conchae stimulation left pupil dilation, cortisol and cardiac vagal activity unchanged [145]. *Protocol-dependent*: a Bayesian meta-analysis of 18 studies found an effect with pulsed delivery (g = 0.36) and evidence for the null with continuous delivery (g = 0.002) [146]. *Weak proxy*: locus coeruleus recording with pupillometry in mice gave a variable, state-dependent mapping [144].

**What the evidence turns on** — whether the waveform is phasic or tonic, and whether pupil diameter measures noradrenergic activity or only correlates with it [144,146]. Section 12.5.

<!-- synthesis -->
**What would settle it** — a human study with a read-out validated against direct noradrenergic measurement, pulsed against continuous delivery within participants [145,146].

### 14.14 Ketamine's active species (H-hnk-active-species)

**The question** — Is (2R,6R)-hydroxynorketamine the antidepressant species, and does it have a target?

**The positions** — *Yes*: in mice the metabolite acted without NMDA receptor inhibition and required AMPA receptor activation [117]. *No target found*: screens across thousands of human proteins found no direct interaction with any proposed ketamine target, though the compound stayed behaviourally active [167]. *A shared downstream step*: in mice, NMDA antagonist pretreatment abolished the effects of ketamine, the metabolite and two unrelated compounds alike [168]; an earlier account routes it through eEF2 kinase [116].

**What the evidence turns on** — whether the assay is receptor binding or pathway blockade, and when the blocker was given [167,168]. Section 11.3.

<!-- synthesis -->
**What would settle it** — matched dosing of the metabolite with and without the downstream block, plus target identification that assumes no high-affinity site [116,167].

### 14.15 5-HT2A necessity (H-5ht2a-necessity)

**The question** — Is postsynaptic 5-HT2A signalling necessary for psychedelic-induced plasticity?

**The positions** — *Postsynaptic 5-HT2A*, the standard account, reviewed with the hallucinogenic and the plasticity question separated [173]. *Presynaptic and afferent*: in knockout mice, lasting strengthening occurred in granular retrosplenial cortex, whose neurons lack postsynaptic 5-HT2A, and needed presynaptic 5-HT2A on thalamic axons [174]. *TrkB*: in mice, psychedelic plasticity required TrkB, not 5-HT2A, while head-twitch required 5-HT2A [119].

**What the evidence turns on** — where the receptor is deleted, and whether the read-out is plasticity or head-twitch; expression is not action [174]. Section 11.4.

<!-- synthesis -->
**What would settle it** — compartment-specific deletion, presynaptic and postsynaptic separately, in several regions; all three positions hold if necessity is regional [119,173,174].

### 14.16 The subjective-experience question (H-subjective-experience)

**The question** — Is the subjective experience necessary for a psychedelic's enduring therapeutic effect?

**The positions** — *Necessary*: with overall drug intensity controlled statistically, specific subjective features predict outcome across human studies, so the neurobiology is necessary but not sufficient [175]. *Not necessary*: the experience-to-outcome link is largely correlational, and lasting changes can occur without it, which is what makes non-hallucinogenic analogues worth building [176].

**What the evidence turns on** — whether the experience can be removed at constant exposure, and whether the trial was blind: expectancy and blinding success go unreported [178]. Section 11.4.

<!-- synthesis -->
**What would settle it** — a trial blocking the subjective effect at constant exposure, with de-blinding measured; one percept carries both mechanism and confound [175,176,178].

### 14.17 Ketamine versus ECT (H-ketamine-vs-ect)

**The question** — Is ketamine non-inferior to electroconvulsive therapy for depression?

**The positions** — *Inferior for remission*: in a randomised open-label non-inferiority trial in hospitalised patients with unipolar depression, remission was 63% with ECT against 46% with thrice-weekly racemic ketamine, so non-inferiority failed [171]. *Non-inferior*: in an open-label randomised non-inferiority trial in 403 patients, response on the QIDS-SR-16 was 55.4% with twice-weekly intravenous ketamine at 0.5 mg/kg against 41.2% with three weeks of ECT, meeting non-inferiority, with early memory decline after ECT that recovered during follow-up [170].

**What the evidence turns on** — setting and endpoint rather than modality: hospitalised against outpatient samples, and clinician-rated remission [171] against self-reported response on the QIDS-SR-16 [170]. Section 13.

<!-- synthesis -->
**What would settle it** — one trial stratified by severity and setting with remission primary in both arms; on present evidence the comparison is conditional on both, not a property of the modalities [170,171].

### 14.18 Intranasal direct transport (H-nose-to-brain)

**The question** — Does intranasal dosing reach the brain other than through systemic absorption?

**The positions** — *Yes*: a review of the anatomy sets out olfactory and trigeminal pathways with perineural and perivascular bulk flow as routes by which proteins, gene vectors and cells could reach CNS tissue without first entering the circulation [161]. *Not shown*: of roughly 100 mostly animal studies, twelve met explicit pharmacokinetic criteria and two, both in rats, indicated direct transport; no human study showed more drug reaching brain targets than intravenous dosing at matched exposure [162].

**What the evidence turns on** — mechanism against pharmacokinetics: an anatomical route [161] is not a measured route effect, which needs plasma and brain exposure taken together with a matched intravenous arm [162]. Section 11.1.

<!-- synthesis -->
**What would settle it** — paired intranasal and intravenous dosing at matched systemic exposure with a central read-out in humans; since the route exists, the quantity in dispute is the direct fraction [161,162].

### 14.19 Receptor-occupancy thresholds (H-d2-occupancy)

**The question** — Is there a universal receptor-occupancy therapeutic window?

**The positions** — *A threshold*: in 22 first-episode patients randomised to haloperidol 1.0 or 2.5 mg/day, raclopride PET put occupancy between 38% and 87% and predicted improvement, with response, extrapyramidal symptoms and hyperprolactinaemia becoming likelier above about 65%, 72% and 78% occupancy [26]. *It breaks for partial agonists*: in a dose–response meta-analysis of 110 studies and 37,193 participants, extrapyramidal risk rose steeply above roughly 75–85% occupancy for antagonists, while partial agonists such as aripiprazole kept that risk low at high occupancy [28].

**What the evidence turns on** — whether occupancy is treated as dose on its own or paired with intrinsic efficacy, and whether the sample contains partial agonists at all [26,28]. Section 3.

<!-- synthesis -->
**What would settle it** — occupancy read out with a pharmacodynamic term beside it, in one design spanning antagonists and partial agonists; a window that holds only for antagonists is a property of the drug class, not of occupancy [26,28].

### 14.20 The serotonin premise (H-serotonin-premise)

**The question** — Does the serotonin-deficit premise behind serotonergic antidepressants hold?

**The positions** — *No deficit evidence*: an umbrella review of 17 systematic reviews and large cohort or genetic studies found no association between serotonin metabolites and depression, weak transporter-binding evidence, and no genotype-by-stress interaction in the largest studies (n = 115,257 and n = 43,165) [188]. *Function-based*: a review holds that a purely transmitter-level account cannot explain the delay to clinical onset or the reversal of heterogeneous symptoms, and locates the action in early changes to emotional and social information processing and in drug-induced plasticity, with no deficit required [187].

**What the evidence turns on** — whether the claim under test is about the disease or about the drug [188]. Section 11.

<!-- synthesis -->
**What would settle it** — nothing as posed: the positions answer different questions, and a drug can work without its putative target deficit existing [188].

### 14.21 The origin of the TMS-evoked potential (H-tep-origin)

**The question** — How much of the TMS-evoked EEG potential is transcranial rather than sensory?

**The positions** — *Largely sensory*: a realistic sham reproducing the auditory and somatosensory effects of TMS evoked EEG responses resembling those to real TMS, early and late, despite noise masking [202]. *Separable*: an optimised sham with a stacked second coil and scalp electrical stimulation left early components and beta responses only with real TMS [203].

**What the evidence turns on** — how good the sham is, and whether real and sham are compared by subtraction or by component identification [202,203]. Section 12.4.

<!-- synthesis -->
**What would settle it** — both sham procedures applied to the same participants with pre-registered component definitions; a mostly sensory potential fits both [202,203].

### 14.22 Magnetogenetics (H-magnetogenetics)

**The question** — Can magnetic fields gate ferritin-tethered ion channels as reported?

**The positions** — *Yes*: a TRPV4–ferritin fusion was reported to permit magnetic control of neuronal activity in imaging, slice and in vivo electrophysiology, and in zebrafish and mouse behaviour [95]. *Physically impossible*: a first-principles analysis found the required forces, torques and heating exceed what paramagnetic ferritin can supply by five to ten orders of magnitude [94].

**What the evidence turns on** — an order-of-magnitude feasibility calculation against a biological demonstration; the dispute is about the coupling term, not replication [94,95]. Section 5.8.

<!-- synthesis -->
**What would settle it** — nothing biological: the objection is dimensional, so only a revised coupling mechanism with its own energy budget engages it [94,95].

### 14.23 The chemogenetic actuator (H-dreadd-actuator)

**The question** — Is clozapine-N-oxide inert at targets other than the designer receptor?

**The positions** — *Inert by design*: directed evolution in yeast, then reconstitution in human cells, produced muscarinic receptors activated by clozapine-N-oxide and not by acetylcholine, and a Gi-coupled designer receptor silenced hippocampal neurons — specificity resting on the ligand being pharmacologically inert [93]. *Acting through clozapine*: PET and pharmacology in rodents and macaques showed that systemically injected clozapine-N-oxide does not enter the brain and binds the receptor weakly; it is converted in vivo to clozapine, which enters and occupies it, and subthreshold clozapine doses preferentially produced the designer-receptor behaviour [29].

**What the evidence turns on** — whether brain entry and receptor occupancy of the administered compound were measured or assumed, and whether a ligand-only control arm was run [29,93]. Section 5.6.

<!-- synthesis -->
**What would settle it** — occupancy measured for the administered compound and its metabolites in the same animals, with a ligand-only arm; the administered compound and the acting ligand are separate objects and belong in separate nodes [29,93].

### 14.24 What the catalogue implies for the graph

<!-- synthesis -->
The entries have a shape the graph must accommodate. In almost none is one side simply wrong: the rivals are two claims measured differently — a modelled threshold against a behavioural effect [45,48], a binding assay against a pathway block [167,168], a retrospective site correlation against a prospective patient predictor [129,130] — so deleting either loses the information that the disagreement is about method. A dispute has to be a first-class object with its rival claims attached, each carrying its own species, preparation and evidence kind, and an edge's status has to be able to say contested while both parents stay in the graph [94,151,202]. Several disputes resolve into dose rather than mechanism once parameters are recorded [84,146,171], which is visible only if the parameter set travels with the claim.

## 15. From this text to a graph

<!-- framing -->
This section gathers what earlier sections asked of a schema, maps it onto existing ontologies and prior graphs, proposes a starter schema, and closes the volume with how to read its literature.

### 15.1 What the earlier sections demand of a schema

<!-- framing -->
The requirements below are stated as a numbered list, each pointing at the section that raised it. None is a new claim; each is the schema consequence that section argued for.

<!-- synthesis -->
1. **Nominal and effective stimulus are separate nodes, not one node with a flag** (sections 2, 3, 5). What the operator set and what reached the tissue can differ in magnitude and, for drugs, in identity: the injected compound is sometimes not the molecule that binds [29].
2. **Dose is a named parameter set with units, one set per drive class** (section 3). Each parameter carries unit and provenance, a missing one is recorded as missing, and any summary metric names its measure; incomplete reporting is a documented field-level defect [79].
3. **Free-field and in situ values are two properties of an acoustic drive** (section 3), as are device settings and modelled field for electromagnetic drives, and administered amount, plasma exposure and target-site exposure for drugs [29].
4. **Coupling edges carry geometry and neural element** (section 4): field orientation relative to the somato-dendritic axis, the element and its class, magnitude with unit and provenance, and whether the claim is initiation or modulation of ongoing activity.
5. **Transducers are nodes, and rival transducers are rival edges between the same two nodes** (section 5), each with its own preparation and its own discriminating test. Receptor compartment is an attribute of the coupling node, because the same agonist at the same receptor acts differently inside the cell and at the surface [92].
6. **Plasticity edges carry the induction protocol with its parameters, the readout, and the preparation and species** (section 6). Direction is a distribution over participants, with responder fraction and predictors, rather than a sign.
7. **Molecular endpoint nodes carry the assay** (section 7), with preparation, species and direction; without them, edges converging on the node are not comparable.
8. **Circuit edges carry the target definition, the state and the biomarker** (section 8). Anatomical, functional and connectivity-derived targets are different nodes; circuits inferred from lesions and from stimulation have been treated as one object, which is checkable only if the definition travels with the node [131]. A necessity edge onto a neuromodulator system carries the perturbation method, and cannot treat the system as a uniform broadcast: the locus coeruleus has target-specific projections [139].
9. **Direction belongs on autonomic edges and sign belongs on the branch** (section 9). The vagus is a bundle of genetically distinct afferent classes [157], and the graph must admit non-neuronal relay nodes: choline acetyltransferase-expressing T cells between vagal signal and macrophage [150], and epithelial sensor cells that synapse onto vagal afferents [158].
10. **Drug drives carry route, exposure profile, compartment reached and active species as properties, not annotations** (section 11), because the same molecule by two routes is two drives and the acting ligand may be a metabolite [29,92].
11. **Every edge carries its confound controls, and confounds are nodes** (section 12): sham type, blinding checks, sensory matching, engagement shown. A confound node has edges to the claims it threatens and counter-edges for rebuttals, and a pooled estimate records the confounds its inputs carried [79].
12. **Evidence type, certainty, species and preparation sit on every edge** (sections 9, 13). A curated projection and a sham-controlled trial are not the same kind of fact, hence an evidence type [218] and a certainty grade [208].
13. **Dose-qualified, state-qualified edges** (sections 2, 6, 8). Sign of effect can invert with dose and with the state at delivery, so the sign belongs on an edge qualified by both, never on the drive node.
14. **Mechanism is a path, not an edge** (section 2). The schema makes a path first-class, so that rival paths between the same endpoints can coexist [29,79].

### 15.2 Which ontologies anchor which level

Uberon is a species-neutral anatomy ontology with more than 6,500 classes that links species-specific anatomy ontologies, so a target or an end organ can be named once and compared across humans and model organisms [214]. Cell types take identifiers from the Cell Ontology, an OBO Foundry ontology of canonical cell types that large consortia require as metadata [215]. Neuron types are better handled compositionally: the Neuron Phenotype Ontology decomposes a neuron type into constituent phenotypes expressed with identifiers from community ontologies, and in testing it reconciled three published cortical neuron classifications with each other and with the informal names used in papers [156].

Molecular and subcellular nodes take Gene Ontology terms for molecular function, cellular component and biological process [216], and chemical entities — drugs, ligands, neurotransmitters — take ChEBI identifiers [217]. Evidence is typed with the Evidence and Conclusion Ontology, a community ontology of evidence types used by dozens of biomedical databases to record how an assertion is supported [218], and certainty is graded with GRADE, introduced as a common system for rating quality of evidence and strength of recommendations [208]. Receptor pharmacology has a settled conceptual vocabulary — affinity, occupancy, efficacy, the operational model, ligand-selective active states, allosteric mechanisms — for ligand–receptor edges [91].

For autonomic connectivity, SCKAN is the substrate: a curated, ontology-backed record of the origins, terminations and routing of autonomic projections between the central nervous system and end organs, assembled from expert input, published literature, textbooks and SPARC datasets, and used to generate the SPARC portal's connectivity maps [155]. Its limit matters as much as its coverage: it holds population-level structural connectivity, not physiology [155]. It says that a population projects by a given route, not what a stimulation train does to that population's firing; that remains a separate claim with its own parameters and species.

<!-- synthesis -->
Protein-level identity — a specific receptor subunit or channel isoform as an entity distinct from its gene — needs a protein ontology alongside these. The corpus for this volume contains no reference describing one, so the schema below leaves the protein slot named but unanchored [215,216,217].

### 15.3 Where nothing exists: protocols and devices

<!-- synthesis -->
No ontology in this volume's corpus covers stimulation protocols or device parameters adequately. The nearest things are reporting standards and repositories, which say what to write down but supply no identifiers or class hierarchy: the RATES Delphi consensus lists 66 items in five groups — participants, stimulator, electrodes, current, procedure — with a 26-item essential subset [193], and the Iowa-Newcastle ultrasound repository holds parameters and outcomes as an open table whose own authors name incomplete parameter reporting as a limit on what can be asked of it [79]. A protocol module therefore has to be authored rather than reused.

<!-- synthesis -->
Its required slots follow from sections 3, 4, 5 and 12: drive class; device and its configuration, including electrode or coil or transducer geometry; waveform with amplitude, pulse width, frequency, phase and polarity; envelope, duty cycle and sonication or train duration; session timing, number of sessions and inter-session interval; free-field and in situ or modelled delivered quantity, each with its unit, its estimation pipeline and whether it was measured or modelled; a summary metric that names the measure it uses; safety quantities with the rule they are judged against; and, for drugs, route, schedule, formulation, exposure measure and active species [29,79,193]. Each slot admits a "not reported" null distinct from zero.

### 15.4 Prior art for the shape

The closest precedent is DrugMechDB, a manually curated database that represents drug mechanisms as paths through a biomedical knowledge graph — 4,583 drug-indication pairs and 32,249 relationships, built as a benchmark for graph-based drug repositioning [223]. Its unit of curation is a hand-assembled path from intervention to indication, not a single triple, which is the right shape; it does not carry route, exposure, per-step species or dispute.

The large integrative graphs supply the engineering patterns. Hetionet combined 29 public resources into 47,031 nodes and 2,250,197 relationships of 24 types, and showed that path patterns through a typed heterogeneous graph carry mechanistic signal usable for prediction [219]. SPOKE links roughly 27 million nodes and 53 million edges of 55 types from 41 databases, organised by 11 ontologies, with programmatic access [221]. PrimeKG merges 20 resources into a multiscale graph spanning 17,080 diseases and 4,050,249 relationships from proteins and pathways to phenotypes and drugs, including indication, contraindication and off-label edge types [222]. SemMedDB shows the alternative acquisition route: subject-predicate-object predications extracted automatically by SemRep from all of PubMed [220].

<!-- synthesis -->
Where these would lose what this volume needs is in edge semantics. An edge typed only by predicate and endpoints cannot hold a parameter set with units, a nominal-versus-effective pair, a preparation, a confound-control record, or a rival edge that is a competing explanation rather than a duplicate assertion. Automatic extraction compounds the problem, because a mined triple inherits the sentence's ambiguity rather than the study's design [220], which is the argument for curating claims by hand as SCKAN and DrugMechDB do [155,223].

### 15.5 A proposed starter schema

<!-- framing -->
What follows is a starting point, not a specification. It is offered in the expectation that a reader building on this volume will redesign it against their own use case.

Every claim edge, whatever its predicate, carries the same four properties: evidence type [218], certainty [208], species and preparation, and a link to its source, plus the dose and state that qualify its sign. An edge without these is not importable [155,218].

### 15.6 How to read this literature

<!-- framing -->
What follows is advice for the reader going to the sources; the builder's own judgement is marked as such.

<!-- synthesis -->
Consensus and reporting documents are the most efficient entry point to a modality, because they name the parameters a field has agreed matter: RATES, from a three-round Delphi study with 38 experts [193], and GRADE for certainty of evidence [208]. No single journal carries this literature. In this volume's corpus the transducer and relay papers appeared in general journals such as *Science* [29,92,150,158], the reporting standard in *Nature Protocols* [193], the ultrasound repository in *eLife* [79], the level reviews in *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* and *Neuron* [139,157], and the ontologies and graphs in data and informatics journals [155,156,222,223]. Following one modality's specialist journal misses most of these.

<!-- synthesis -->
Searching by modality name returns the recent tier. Three strategies in this volume's sweep returned the seminal tier instead. First, search by the dispute rather than the technique — name the two rivals and the question, as the hypothesis groups in this volume do, and the founding papers appear on both sides. Second, walk citations backwards from a consensus document or a systematic review to the primary studies it grades, then forwards from a founding primary study to the paper that reinterpreted it. Third, search for the mechanism at the level below the one you care about: the ChAT-expressing T cell relay [150] and the epithelial sensor cell synapse [158] are found under immunology and gut physiology, not under nerve stimulation, and the intracellular receptor result [92] under receptor biology rather than under psychedelics.

<!-- synthesis -->
Four kinds of result deserve scepticism on the evidence collected here. A pooled human estimate whose authors call it tentative, limited by selection bias and incomplete parameter reporting, as the ultrasound meta-analysis does of itself [79]. A control assumed inert rather than measured, as with the designer ligand that turned out to be converted to a brain-penetrant parent compound [29]. A structural connectivity statement read as a physiological one, which the SCKAN documentation does not license [155]. And an automatically extracted assertion used as a fact, since text-mined predications carry the sentence rather than the study design [220]. In each case the question to ask is the volume's recurring one: what was measured, in what species and preparation, at what dose, and what rival explanation was excluded [155,208,218].
